# Interview 4

- **Topic:** Completionism, Collection, Game-Related Activities, Autonomy, and Long-Term Engagement

## English Transcript

**Interviewer:** First, I need your consent to record this interview. Your voice and the interview data will only be used for our university coursework.

**Interviewee:** I agree.

**Interviewer:** Do you usually play games? What types of games do you play most often, and roughly how much time do you spend on them?

**Interviewee:** Yes, I play games every day. I would say I normally spend at least two hours a day playing. I play ARPGs, turn-based games, and monster-collecting or creature-raising games. At the moment I am playing *Roco Kingdom World*, which also includes some social elements.

**Interviewer:** What would you say is your favourite type of game?

**Interviewee:** Probably Metroidvania games.

**Interviewer:** What attracts you to that genre?

**Interviewee:** Usually they have collection elements, interconnected maps, exploration, and different abilities that gradually unlock areas. I am quite completionist when I play games, so I really like the feeling of collecting everything. Even revealing the entire map counts as a kind of collection to me. What I enjoy is that feeling of not repeating things unnecessarily, not missing anything, and eventually obtaining or discovering everything that the game contains.

**Interviewer:** Within the idea of “Metroidvania,” do you think you prefer the *Metroid* side, which focuses more on interconnected maps and ability-based exploration, or the *Castlevania* side, which usually includes more combat, equipment, and collection?

**Interviewee:** Probably the *Castlevania* side. In terms of setting, I prefer gothic or darker fantasy themes more than science fiction. I also like the stronger collection elements. So aesthetically and mechanically, I probably lean more toward the *Castlevania* side.

**Interviewer:** What about a game like *Animal Well*? It has interconnected exploration and ability-based progression, but very little traditional combat.

**Interviewee:** I still enjoyed *Animal Well*. It has action elements and platforming, but not really conventional combat. The puzzle design and exploration are extremely good. So even though I say I like combat and collection, I can still enjoy a game without much combat if the exploration and puzzle design are strong enough. The problem is that *Animal Well* is probably the only game of that specific type that I have played, and it is unusually good, so I cannot confidently say that I would enjoy every non-combat Metroidvania.

**Interviewer:** So it is not that every individual element is essential. A particularly strong game can compensate for the absence of something like combat?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I like a combination of exploration, collecting, progression, and combat, but if one part is missing and the remaining parts are exceptionally well designed, I can still enjoy it.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned completionism. How strongly does that affect the way you actually play?

**Interviewee:** Quite strongly. I spend a lot of time checking guides, making plans, and making sure I do not miss things. For example, if there is an item, achievement, or event that can permanently be missed, I can feel very uncomfortable if I realise I missed it. Sometimes it can make me want to reload an earlier save or even restart the game.

**Interviewer:** What if the missed content has almost no effect on the story or gameplay? For example, maybe an NPC says one different line at the end of the game, but nothing substantial changes.

**Interviewee:** I can still feel uncomfortable because then it is no longer “complete.” Even if the difference is tiny, I know that I missed something. Achievements are similar. If I know I have permanently missed an achievement, I can feel very bad about it.

**Interviewer:** Do you always pursue 100% completion, then?

**Interviewee:** Not always. There is another side to it. If I realise that some achievements are simply too difficult or would require an unreasonable amount of effort, I can give up. For example, some games have hundreds of collectibles or extremely difficult challenge achievements. At some point I can say, “Forget it, this is not worth doing.” I think my attitude is that I want to be around a 70-point player rather than a perfect 100-point player. I want to complete the things that feel achievable and meaningful, but if the game becomes too difficult to fully control, I may stop trying to complete everything.

**Interviewer:** Is that why games with extremely deep systems can sometimes make you uncomfortable?

**Interviewee:** Yes. If there are too many systems and I feel that I cannot fully understand or control them, I can become intimidated. I played *The Binding of Isaac* for a while, for example, but there were so many items, interactions, and things to learn that I felt I could never completely understand it. The depth itself became a little frightening. I had a similar feeling with *Don't Starve*. The learning curve felt very steep. I think I could probably enjoy it if someone experienced introduced me to it, but learning everything alone felt overwhelming.

**Interviewer:** So you enjoy depth, but only up to the point where you still feel that the system can eventually be understood and managed?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I want a certain amount of depth, but not an infinite amount. I want to feel that I can eventually understand the system and have some control over it.

**Interviewer:** Can you describe a time when you played for a particularly long period?

**Interviewee:** When I played *Hollow Knight*, I remember spending an entire evening fighting one boss, Nightmare King Grimm. I think I spent around three hours just trying to defeat that boss. But the longest sessions were probably with *The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild*. When I was exploring and finding shrines, I could play for an entire day. At my most extreme, I probably played around fifteen hours in one day.

**Interviewer:** What kept you playing for fifteen hours? Was there one specific reward?

**Interviewee:** Not necessarily one reward. With a game like *Breath of the Wild*, there is always another place to explore, another shrine, or another thing to discover. You keep moving from one small goal to another. Because exploration itself is enjoyable, there is not always an obvious moment where you feel you should stop.

**Interviewer:** Do you usually prefer playing alone or with other people?

**Interviewee:** Definitely alone. I am basically a single-player person.

**Interviewer:** Why?

**Interviewee:** Playing with other people gives me pressure. Because I am quite perfectionistic, when I play a game I often want to stop and check guides, plan things, examine my inventory, or make sure I have not missed something. If I am playing with another person, I have to consider their pace. Maybe they want to keep moving while I want to stop and read something. Maybe there is a cutscene that I want to watch completely, while they want to skip it. So multiplayer means I have to compromise, and that can make me uncomfortable.

**Interviewer:** Is performance pressure also part of it?

**Interviewee:** Yes. If I play a multiplayer game and I perform badly, I become frustrated with myself. My friends do not even need to criticise me. I can pressure myself. I am quite competitive when the game involves winning and losing, so if I feel that I am dragging the team down, I become annoyed. That is why I prefer playing alone. It is calmer, and I can control the pace.

**Interviewer:** Are there any multiplayer games you do enjoy?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I really like *Kingdom Two Crowns* with another person because it has local cooperative play and the cooperation feels relaxed. I do not feel the same kind of competitive pressure.

**Interviewer:** Has gaming ever seriously interfered with something important in your life?

**Interviewee:** Yes, there have been a few cases. One of the clearest examples happened when I was preparing for IELTS. The night before the test, I was playing games. My mother reminded me to bring my identification, but the next morning I forgot it. When I arrived at the test centre, they would not let me in, so that whole test opportunity was wasted.

Another time, I was doing an internship at a food factory. I had recently bought a lot of games on Steam and decided to try one before going to sleep. The game happened to be *Kingdom Two Crowns*. I kept playing until around five in the morning even though I had to start work at eight. In the end I slept until around ten and had to take time off.

**Interviewer:** So in those cases, gaming directly affected sleep and important responsibilities?

**Interviewee:** Yes. Those were quite clear examples. The consequences were not catastrophic, but they definitely affected my attention, sleep, and plans.

**Interviewer:** I also want to ask about time spent on game-related activities outside the game itself. Some players spend huge amounts of time on guides, tools, mods, databases, translations, or other things connected to games. You have done similar things before, right?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I think game-related activities can produce the same kind of flow as actually playing. For example, I once worked on a fan translation for a game. I only played the game itself for around thirty hours, but I spent more than 200 hours on the translation.

During that period, I could wake up, start working on the translation, and continue until late at night. I did not want to stop or sleep. That feeling was actually very similar to being absorbed in a game.

**Interviewer:** What made the translation so engaging if it was not actually gameplay?

**Interviewee:** I think it was the process itself. I was working on something, making progress, solving problems, and seeing the project gradually become complete. That can create a strong sense of flow. Games are only one way of getting that kind of satisfaction. Other activities can do the same thing.

**Interviewer:** Did spending more than 200 hours on the translation affect your studies at the time?

**Interviewee:** Definitely. I keep records of how much time I spend studying, and I think during that semester the total amount of time I spent studying across four courses may not even have reached 200 hours. There was one whole month where I spent most of my time working on the translation.

**Interviewer:** When you started the translation, was it completely voluntary and enjoyable throughout, or did your motivation change later?

**Interviewee:** It changed. At the beginning, I genuinely liked the game and wanted to make the translation. I enjoyed doing it. But later there were definitely moments when I was frustrated and did not enjoy the process as much.

Even then, I still felt that I needed to continue. Part of it was probably sunk cost because I had already done so much, but I would not say it was only sunk cost. There was also responsibility. Once I had started the project and other people were expecting it, I felt responsible for finishing it.

**Interviewer:** So something that started as “I want to do this” gradually became partly “I should finish this”?

**Interviewee:** Yes. That definitely happened.

**Interviewer:** Do you think your autonomy decreased at that point?

**Interviewee:** To some extent, yes. I still made the decision to continue, but there were moments where I was not really enjoying it anymore and still felt that I should keep going.

**Interviewer:** Do you have similar experiences with games you currently play?

**Interviewee:** Yes. Right now I am doing a lot of breeding in *Roco Kingdom World*. I built an Excel-based system to manage all the creatures I have. I would not call it a professional database, but it basically functions like one. I have already recorded more than 500 creatures.

I probably spent forty or fifty hours building and maintaining that system.

**Interviewer:** Why spend so much time making a database for a game?

**Interviewee:** Because organising everything gives me a strange kind of satisfaction. When everything around me is arranged neatly and I understand how everything fits together, I feel good. It gives me a sense of control.

**Interviewer:** So the pleasure is not only in collecting the creatures, but also in structuring and managing information about them?

**Interviewee:** Yes. The management itself becomes enjoyable.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever deliberately tried to reduce the amount of time you spend playing games or doing game-related activities?

**Interviewee:** Yes, especially when I was younger. There were times in high school when I felt that I was spending too much time gaming and needed to control it.

**Interviewer:** What methods worked for you?

**Interviewee:** One thing I changed was how I entered the game. Before, I might log in without a clear plan and just wander around, check my inventory, or think about what to do next. That wastes a lot of time.

When I felt more pressure from real-life responsibilities, I started planning before logging in. I would decide exactly what I needed to do that day, log in, complete the necessary daily tasks or collect the resources I needed, and then log out immediately.

That helped reduce the time I spent in the game.

**Interviewer:** So instead of limiting the session with an external timer, you reduced unnecessary time by setting clear goals inside the game?

**Interviewee:** Yes. That works better for me.

**Interviewer:** Do external tools such as timers or screen-time limits work?

**Interviewee:** Not very well. For example, I currently set myself a two-hour daily limit for one activity, but today I have already exceeded two hours and I am still doing it. A timer can tell me that the time is up, but it does not necessarily make me stop.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned earlier that being able to spend a lot of time on games is not always something negative. Can you explain that idea?

**Interviewee:** I actually think being deeply immersed in games can sometimes represent a very fortunate situation.

If you can support yourself, you have enough freedom, you have enough control over your time, and you are not using games simply to escape responsibilities that you clearly need to deal with, then having a period where you can spend a lot of your free time playing games can be a very happy experience.

I think having that kind of freedom is almost a privilege.

**Interviewer:** So if someone deliberately sets aside a period of time specifically to play a game, even extremely intensively, you would not necessarily see that as a problem?

**Interviewee:** Exactly. Imagine someone knows that a new game is coming out and deliberately clears two weeks of their schedule because they want to enjoy it. If they then play for twenty hours a day during that period, I do not automatically think that is problematic. They deliberately created that time for the game. It is their choice.

**Interviewer:** So the amount of gaming time is less important than whether the person actually has control over that time?

**Interviewee:** Yes. If the time genuinely belongs to them and they choose to use it that way, then I do not think high playtime alone is enough to make it a problem.

**Interviewer:** What would make the situation different?

**Interviewee:** If someone has important things they genuinely need or want to do, such as finding a job or preparing for an exam, but they avoid those responsibilities by playing games and assume someone else will always support them, then I think that is very different. That is closer to escaping from things they need to face rather than deliberately using free time.

**Interviewer:** What about live-service games that use daily and weekly tasks? Imagine a player does not actually want to play today, but still logs in because they need resources for a long-term goal. Do you think that is unhealthy?

**Interviewee:** I think it can be unhealthy. Live-service games need active players. They need people to log in, maintain community activity, and keep the server populated, so they have to create reasons for people to return regularly.

That is why they use daily tasks, weekly tasks, stamina systems, and resources that overflow if you do not use them. They intentionally make it inefficient to just log in once a week and complete everything at once.

In that sense, they are manipulating players' time and attention for commercial benefit, and there is a potential cost to the player.

**Interviewer:** Would you say those systems reduce player autonomy?

**Interviewee:** They can, because the game is deciding when you need to return. If you genuinely do not want to play today but still feel that you need to log in because your stamina will overflow or because you will lose a reward, then part of the timing is being controlled by the system.

**Interviewer:** But earlier you also said that long-term goals are not necessarily bad. How do you distinguish those situations?

**Interviewee:** I think it is complicated. Some long-term goals require a lot of repetitive work, but the player may actually enjoy the persistence itself.

For someone who genuinely completes a very difficult long-term goal, the final reward may not be the only source of satisfaction. They may also enjoy thinking, “I persisted,” “I had enough discipline to do this,” or “I completed something most people would not complete.”

That feeling of persistence and achievement can also be part of the fun.

**Interviewer:** So repetitive work does not automatically mean the player is being exploited?

**Interviewee:** Right. It depends on the scale and the design.

**Interviewer:** Can you give an example of a system that you think becomes excessive?

**Interviewee:** There are games where a cosmetic reward or another relatively small item requires an absurd amount of grinding. For example, there might be a resource where you need hundreds of pieces, but each piece requires many matches and is not even guaranteed to drop. You could end up spending months doing the same activity every day.

At that point, I think the design can become malicious or at least deliberately excessive.

**Interviewer:** Why?

**Interviewee:** Because the requirement may not reflect meaningful gameplay anymore. It may just be a number that someone set extremely high to keep players engaged for longer.

If you need to grind every day for half a year for something that has almost no functional value, then a huge amount of time is being consumed just to maintain engagement.

**Interviewer:** Do you think shorter daily systems can sometimes be more dangerous than extremely unrealistic long-term goals?

**Interviewee:** Possibly. If a goal is obviously ridiculous, most players will just give up and say they are not going to do it. They know it is unreasonable.

But something that only asks you to do a small amount every day can be easier to accept. You think, “It is only a little bit today.” Then you repeat that every day for a long time.

That can be a very effective way for a live-service game to keep players returning.

**Interviewer:** So a moderate but persistent requirement may influence behaviour more effectively than an obviously impossible goal?

**Interviewee:** Yes. If the requirement feels just achievable enough, people are more likely to continue doing it.

**Interviewer:** Overall, when you think about gaming addiction or problematic gaming, what matters most to you?

**Interviewee:** I think autonomy matters a lot. Spending a lot of time playing games is not automatically unhealthy if the person has genuinely chosen to use their time that way and their life allows it.

What worries me more is when the player feels that they no longer control the decision: when the system decides when they must log in, when sunk cost or responsibility makes them continue something they no longer enjoy, or when gaming starts replacing things they genuinely want or need to do.

At the same time, even those situations are not completely simple because people can also find meaning and satisfaction in persistence itself. I think you have to look at why they are continuing, not only how many hours they spend.
