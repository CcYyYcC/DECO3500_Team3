# Interview 4

- **Topic:** Completionism, Collection, Game-Related Activities, Autonomy, and Long-Term Engagement

## English Transcript

**Interviewer:** What kinds of games do you usually play, and what type do you enjoy most?

**Interviewee:** I play games every day, usually for more than two hours. I like ARPGs, turn-based games, and especially monster-collecting games. My favourite genre is Metroidvania. What I enjoy most is the combination of interconnected maps, collecting, progression, and completion. Even revealing every part of the map feels like a kind of collection to me. I like the feeling of not missing anything and eventually obtaining everything.

**Interviewer:** Which part of Metroidvania games attracts you most?

**Interviewee:** Probably the *Castlevania* side rather than the *Metroid* side. I like darker fantasy or gothic aesthetics more than science fiction, and I also prefer games with more collection elements. That said, I can still enjoy something like *Animal Well*, which has very little combat, because the exploration and puzzle design are very strong.

**Interviewer:** How does completionism affect the way you play games?

**Interviewee:** Quite strongly. I often look up guides, make plans, and try to avoid missing content. If I miss something important or permanently miss an achievement, I can feel extremely uncomfortable. Sometimes I have even considered restarting a game because of something I missed. However, if an achievement is so difficult that I know I probably cannot get it, I can eventually accept that and stop pursuing 100% completion. I would say I want to be a "70-point player" rather than always reaching 100%.

**Interviewer:** Can you remember times when you played for very long periods?

**Interviewee:** Yes. When I played *The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild*, I could play for around fifteen hours in one day because I kept exploring and completing shrines. When I played *Hollow Knight*, I spent several hours fighting Nightmare King Grimm because I wanted to defeat him.

**Interviewer:** Do you prefer playing alone or with other people?

**Interviewee:** I am mostly a single-player person. Playing with other people gives me pressure. Because I am quite perfectionistic, I like taking my time, checking guides, planning what to do, watching cutscenes, and making sure I do not miss information. If I play with someone else, I may need to compromise or follow their pace. I also become frustrated if I perform badly in a multiplayer game, even if my friends do not criticise me. So single-player games feel calmer and more controllable.

**Interviewer:** Has gaming ever seriously interfered with important responsibilities?

**Interviewee:** Yes. Once I was preparing for IELTS and played games the night before the test. My mother reminded me to bring my identification, but the next morning I forgot it and was not allowed to enter the test, so that attempt was wasted. Another time, during an internship at a food factory, I started playing *Kingdom Two Crowns* at night and continued until around 5 a.m. I was supposed to start work at 8 a.m., but I slept until around 10 and had to take time off.

**Interviewer:** You also spend a lot of time on activities related to games rather than just playing them. Why?

**Interviewee:** I think game-related activities can produce the same kind of flow. For example, I once spent more than 200 hours working on a fan translation for a game, even though I only played the game itself for around thirty hours. During that period, I could wake up, start working on the translation, and continue until late at night without wanting to stop. It felt similar to being deeply absorbed in a game.

**Interviewer:** Did that affect your studies, and did your motivation change over time?

**Interviewee:** Definitely. I track my study time, and during that semester, the total time I spent studying across four courses may not even have reached 200 hours. During one month in particular, I spent most of my time working on the translation. At first, I did it because I liked the game and wanted to contribute. Later, there were points where the process became frustrating and less enjoyable, but I still felt that I should finish because I had already done so much and because I felt some responsibility toward the project. It was not only sunk cost; responsibility also became part of it.

**Interviewer:** Do you have similar experiences with current games?

**Interviewee:** Yes. In *Roco Kingdom World*, I spend a lot of time on breeding. I created an Excel-based database to manage the creatures and have recorded more than 500 of them. I probably spent forty or fifty hours just building and maintaining that system. Organising everything neatly gives me a strange sense of satisfaction. It is not only about the game itself but also about creating order and being able to understand and manage the system.

**Interviewer:** Have you tried to control the amount of time you spend gaming?

**Interviewee:** Yes. Earlier in my life, when I felt I was spending too much time on games, I started planning what I needed to do before logging in. Instead of entering the game and wandering around without a goal, I would decide exactly which daily tasks or resources I needed, complete them, and then leave. That reduced wasted time. External timers do not work very well for me. For example, I currently have a two-hour daily limit for one activity, but I have already exceeded it today.

**Interviewer:** How do you personally think about very high levels of gaming?

**Interviewee:** I actually think there can be something fortunate about having a period of life where you are able to immerse yourself in games. If you can support yourself, have enough freedom, and are not using games simply to escape responsibilities that you need to face, then being able to spend a large amount of your free time on something you genuinely enjoy can be a privileged and happy state. If someone deliberately clears two weeks to play a new game and spends most of those two weeks playing, I do not think that is automatically a problem. They deliberately chose that experience.

**Interviewer:** What about daily tasks and long-term goals in online games, where players may not want to log in today but feel that they need to?

**Interviewee:** I think that can become unhealthy. Online games need active users, so developers use daily tasks, weekly tasks, stamina systems, and expiring resources to encourage players to return. In that sense, the system is using players' time and attention for commercial reasons. At the same time, long-term goals are complicated. Some people genuinely enjoy the process of persistence itself. They may enjoy feeling that they are working toward something difficult. So I do not think every repetitive long-term goal is automatically bad. The problem becomes more obvious when the requirement is deliberately excessive or when players feel pressured to participate even though the activity itself gives them very little value.
