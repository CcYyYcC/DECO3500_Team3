# Interview 1

- **Topic:** Gaming Habits, Competitive Motivation, Social Play, and Self-Control

## English Transcript

**Interviewer:** First, I need to ask for your consent to record this interview. This interview is for our University of Queensland course project about combating gaming addiction, and the recording will only be used for this coursework.

**Interviewee:** Okay, I agree.

**Interviewer:** To start with, do you usually play games? What kinds of games do you mainly play, and how often do you play them?

**Interviewee:** Yes, I play games. I mainly play competitive games, especially FPS games. Usually I play every one or two days. I would not say I play every single day, because sometimes I have other things to do and simply cannot play. But if I had enough free time and nothing else stopping me, I probably would play every day.

**Interviewer:** What is the most enjoyable part of competitive games for you?

**Interviewee:** The feeling of beating other people. That is probably the most direct answer. In FPS games especially, defeating another player gives me a strong sense of satisfaction.

**Interviewer:** What specific games are you thinking of?

**Interviewee:** Games like *Delta Force* and *Arena Breakout*. Mostly FPS games.

**Interviewer:** There are also many other competitive genres, such as MOBAs or MMORPGs. Why do you prefer FPS games rather than those?

**Interviewee:** To put it simply, those other games do not give me the same satisfying feeling because I realised that I am not particularly talented at them. If I play a game and I just keep getting beaten, I do not enjoy it very much. With FPS games, I already had some experience and some ability, so they are easier for me to enjoy.

**Interviewer:** So your own performance in the game affects whether you want to continue playing it?

**Interviewee:** Yes. If I am good at it, I enjoy it. If I keep getting beaten and feel that I am not good at it, I lose interest.

**Interviewer:** Can you talk about your experience with a specific FPS game, for example *Delta Force*? How did you start playing it, and how did your experience change over time?

**Interviewee:** When *Delta Force* first came out, I had already played other FPS games before, so I adapted to it quite quickly. At the beginning I felt that I was playing quite well, so the game was comfortable and enjoyable for me. Later, as people became more familiar with the mechanics and everyone had played for longer, the difference between players became smaller. I started finding it harder to beat other players. When I realised I was losing more often, I gradually played less.

**Interviewer:** I also want to understand what a single gaming session looks like for you. Imagine a day when you have a lot of free time. From the moment you decide to start playing until you eventually stop, what usually determines how long you continue?

**Interviewee:** If I have a lot of free time and there is nothing else I need to do, I can just keep playing. Usually I stop when I become tired. If I know playing will not affect anything I have to do afterwards, then I do not really have another reason to stop.

**Interviewer:** What if you know you have something the next morning, such as work or another responsibility?

**Interviewee:** Then that changes things. If I know that continuing will affect what I have to do the next day, I am more likely to stop. But if I feel that there will not be any real consequence, I can continue for much longer.

**Interviewer:** Do you normally prefer playing with friends, with people you already know, or playing solo?

**Interviewee:** I prefer playing with friends. Playing with random teammates often means there is not much coordination, and sometimes you meet people who put a lot of pressure on you. If I play with friends whose personalities I already know and get along with, the whole experience is more comfortable.

**Interviewer:** Compared with playing alone, what does playing with friends add to the experience?

**Interviewee:** Mainly coordination and familiarity. When you know the people you are playing with, you understand how they behave and communicate. With random players, you do not know what kind of person you will meet. Some people can be difficult or stressful to play with.

**Interviewer:** If you are playing with friends and one of them says, “I have something tomorrow, so I am stopping at nine,” while you originally planned to play until midnight, what would you usually do?

**Interviewee:** Usually if everyone stops playing, I stop too. I might just lie in bed and use my phone afterwards.

**Interviewer:** So you normally would not continue playing by yourself?

**Interviewee:** Not usually, but there are exceptions. Sometimes I do play alone when my friends are not online. If I have not played for a while, or if I really want to play and nobody else is available, then I might log in and play by myself for a while.

**Interviewer:** Suppose you and your friends planned to play together later, but you realised that your in-game resources or money were not enough. Would you log in early and grind by yourself to prepare?

**Interviewee:** It depends. If I really want to play that game with them, I might prepare with them instead. If I do not especially want to play it by myself, I would probably find another game to play rather than force myself to grind alone.

**Interviewer:** Can you remember a specific occasion when you played for a very long time, much longer than usual?

**Interviewee:** Yes. There was one time when I kept losing badly. I just kept playing and kept playing because I was frustrated. I felt that I had already played this type of game for such a long time, so I could not accept losing that badly. I felt unwilling to stop after such a poor performance. I kept thinking I should be able to do better, and eventually I played through the whole night.

**Interviewer:** Roughly how many hours did you play that time?

**Interviewee:** Definitely more than ten hours.

**Interviewer:** This is interesting because earlier you said that if you play another type of competitive game and keep losing, you may simply stop because you feel you are not good at it. But in FPS games, losing can make you continue for even longer. What do you think creates that difference?

**Interviewee:** I think the main difference is that I had already experienced success in FPS games before. I had already “tasted success,” so to speak. In some other games, I never really had that experience. If I have never been good at a game, then losing does not really make me want to prove anything. I just think I am not good at it and stop playing.

**Interviewer:** But in FPS games, because you have previously been good at them, losing feels different?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I have played games like Counter-Strike for a long time. So if I suddenly get beaten badly, I feel like I should not be performing that badly. That makes me more unwilling to accept the result.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever had a situation where playing games affected something you originally planned to do?

**Interviewee:** Yes, although I would not say the consequences have been extremely serious. Sometimes I get too absorbed and stay up too late, and then when I go to work the next day I do not have much energy. My condition is worse and I feel more tired.

**Interviewer:** If you imagine your life without gaming, do you think your work or daily life would be significantly different?

**Interviewee:** Probably not significantly. I do not think gaming has had such a severe impact on my life that everything would be completely different without it.

**Interviewer:** Have there been situations where you clearly knew that you should not be playing, but you still wanted to play?

**Interviewee:** Yes. For example, right now I am not working, and I am researching and working on something important. I know that during this period I should not really be spending much time playing games. So I moved my computer to my sister's shop. I started going there every day almost like going to work. That way, when I came back home at night, the computer was not there and I could not easily start playing.

**Interviewer:** So you deliberately changed your environment to make gaming less accessible?

**Interviewee:** Yes. That was the idea. Recently I moved the computer back to where I live because I feel that the thing I was working on is almost finished, so now I might allow myself to play a bit more again.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever finished a gaming session and regretted how long you played, because it disrupted your plans?

**Interviewee:** I have definitely felt some regret. Sometimes I think I played longer than I should have. But so far I have not experienced consequences serious enough to make me feel that gaming has completely damaged something important in my life.

**Interviewer:** Have you tried other ways of controlling how long you play?

**Interviewee:** Usually I have a general idea that by around one or two in the morning I should stop and sleep. Most of the time I can do that. Occasionally I still stay up all night.

**Interviewer:** When you stop at that time, are you using any particular tool, or do you simply decide that it is time to stop?

**Interviewee:** Usually I just stop normally and go to sleep. I do not really use a special tool for that.

**Interviewer:** Compared with that, moving your computer to your sister's shop sounds like a much stronger form of control.

**Interviewee:** Yes. That is more effective because it physically changes whether I can play. It is not just relying on telling myself to stop.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever deliberately reduced or completely stopped playing a particular type of game because you thought it was taking too much time, costing too much money, or interfering with your life?

**Interviewee:** Not really. Usually when I stop playing a game, it is because I am no longer interested in it. For example, I used to play *Minecraft* a lot, but after playing it for a long time, I gradually felt that it was no longer fresh or interesting. Then other games became popular, so I moved on to those instead.

**Interviewer:** So most of the time the decision comes from whether you still find the game enjoyable, rather than consciously forcing yourself to stop?

**Interviewee:** Yes, basically. If I like it, I play it. If I stop finding it interesting, I stop.

**Interviewer:** What about money? Have you ever liked a game but deliberately stopped because you thought it would cost too much?

**Interviewee:** Not really. I do not spend heavily on games. I might spend a small amount sometimes, but not enough for money to become a major reason to quit.

**Interviewer:** What originally made you start playing *Delta Force*? Was it mainly the gameplay itself, marketing, or the fact that people around you were playing?

**Interviewee:** More because people around me were playing it.

**Interviewer:** So social influence was quite important?

**Interviewee:** Yes. A lot of the games I have played were games that people around me were also playing.

**Interviewer:** We once played another extraction-style mobile game together, but you did not continue playing it afterwards. Why?

**Interviewee:** Mainly because I do not like playing that kind of game on a phone. I do not like the controls on mobile devices.

**Interviewer:** So if the format or control method does not feel right, even social influence is not enough to keep you playing?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I still need to actually like the way the game feels.

**Interviewer:** Looking back at the games you have played, do you feel that you are mainly driven by instinctive preference? For example, if you enjoy something, you keep playing it, and if you do not enjoy it, you simply stop?

**Interviewee:** Yes, that is pretty accurate. I have played many games for a period of time and then stopped. If my friends stop playing a game, I often play it less as well. There are not many games that I keep playing permanently.

**Interviewer:** Besides FPS games and extraction shooters, are there other types of games that you have been strongly engaged with?

**Interviewee:** I also like survival and crafting games, where you collect resources and build things.

**Interviewer:** What do those games give you that is similar to FPS games?

**Interviewee:** I realised that I like them partly because I feel that I can make things that look better than what other people make. That gives me satisfaction. It is actually similar to FPS games. If I feel that I am good at something, I enjoy it. If I feel that I am bad at it and cannot perform well, I am much less interested in continuing.

**Interviewer:** So whether it is competitive performance in an FPS or building something in a survival game, part of the enjoyment comes from feeling competent at what you are doing?

**Interviewee:** Yes, I think so.
