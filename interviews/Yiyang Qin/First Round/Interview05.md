# Interview 5

- **Topic:** FPS Games, Social Gaming, Leisure Choices, and Definitions of Addiction

## English Transcript

**Interviewer:** How often do you play games, what types do you prefer, and what do you enjoy most?

**Interviewee:** I play every day, probably around three to eight hours. My favourite type is FPS games. The thing I enjoy most is the feedback from getting a kill. In an FPS game, defeating another player already feels satisfying, and when the game provides strong visual or audio feedback for the kill, it reinforces that feeling.

**Interviewer:** Is the kill itself the main source of enjoyment, or are the tactics and interactions leading up to it also important?

**Interviewee:** The tactics, game knowledge, and coordination with teammates are the things that make the kill possible. So if someone says that the interesting part of an FPS is gathering information, making decisions, coordinating, using abilities or utility, and then eventually producing the kill, I agree with that. I just describe the final kill feedback as the most obvious moment of satisfaction.

**Interviewer:** Do you prefer playing with friends or alone, and what makes gaming different as a social activity?

**Interviewee:** Definitely with friends. For me, gaming has gradually become a form of social activity. Being with people I already know feels more relaxed and natural than playing with random people. Compared with face-to-face socialising, games also require you to think about fewer things. In real life you might think about how you dress, how you behave, and social manners. In a game, a lot of the interaction is mediated through how well you understand and play the game. You can help your friends win, or you can simply have fun together.

**Interviewer:** If you had to choose between performing very well alone and playing casually with friends, which would you prefer?

**Interviewee:** I would rather play casually with friends. Rank and statistics mostly show your level of familiarity with the game. Even if my statistics were very good, I would still rather play badly with friends and lose together than play alone just to maintain good data.

**Interviewer:** You sometimes still play by yourself. What motivates you when the social element is missing?

**Interviewee:** In that case, it is mainly a way to pass time. If I am already at the computer doing something and become tired, opening a game for a while is one possible way to take a break. I could also use my phone, watch something, or do something else. I do not think there has to be one specific way of passing time.

**Interviewer:** Playing an FPS by yourself can also expose you to bad teammates, criticism, or frustrating matches. Why choose that instead of a safer and easier activity like watching short videos?

**Interviewee:** I think it is because of uncertainty. When I scroll through short videos, I am basically repeating the same action. In a multiplayer game, even if I play the same map, the opponents are different, my teammates are different, our strategies are different, and my own performance is different. Every match has many unknown factors. That uncertainty makes me willing to choose something that requires more energy.

**Interviewer:** If the friends you are playing with decide to stop, do you usually continue?

**Interviewee:** Usually I stop as well. It depends on the time and my energy level. If it is still early in the afternoon and I have plenty of energy, I may find another group of people to play with, but I probably would not continue alone. If it is already late and everyone is tired, then when one person suggests stopping, I will normally stop too.

**Interviewer:** Can you remember a day when you played for a particularly long time? What kept you playing?

**Interviewee:** I have definitely had days where I played for more than twelve hours. Usually it happened because my friends were also playing, nobody suggested stopping, and we started quite early, so we had a lot of time available. If I were alone, I probably would not play a very demanding FPS for that long. I would choose something more relaxed instead.

**Interviewer:** What keeps you engaged when you play a more relaxed game alone?

**Interviewee:** For example, in *Minecraft*, I enjoy building things. If I am working on a building, I want to see what it looks like when it is finished, so that keeps me going. There is also still a social element because after building something, I can show it to my friends and get feedback from them.

**Interviewer:** Has gaming ever interfered with something you originally planned to do?

**Interviewee:** Mostly small things. For example, I might plan to eat dinner at six, but if I am playing, I keep delaying it. Gaming usually affects the lowest-priority parts of my schedule rather than important commitments. I have not had a case where gaming seriously damaged something like an examination, major project, or relationship.

**Interviewer:** How do you prevent gaming from interfering with important plans?

**Interviewee:** If I need to go somewhere, I calculate how much time I need to get ready and leave. I give myself a latest possible stopping time. When that time arrives, I stop immediately. It is mainly self-discipline rather than using a special tool.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever deliberately reduced your gaming time?

**Interviewee:** Sometimes, but usually not because I force myself to. If I play a lot for one week, I may naturally feel less interested in gaming the following week. My interest tends to come in phases. A phase might be short or it might last a long time, but I can also return to games that I stopped playing before.

**Interviewer:** Why have FPS games remained appealing to you for so long?

**Interviewee:** Again, I think uncertainty is important. Every match is different. At the same time, the longer you play an FPS, the more familiar you become with aiming, positioning, maps, and game knowledge. Eventually it feels very natural rather than difficult like it does for a new player. It also becomes a habit.

**Interviewer:** If you reduced your gaming time by half, do you think your life would improve?

**Interviewee:** Probably not very much. If I normally played ten hours and reduced that to five, I do not think the other five hours would automatically become studying or exercise. I would probably watch television, watch films, use my phone, or just sit around. Not playing games does not necessarily mean that I would do something more productive.

**Interviewer:** How do you personally define gaming addiction?

**Interviewee:** I think the main issue is whether gaming affects the life you originally intended to have. If it does not interfere with your planned life, I do not think the number of hours alone necessarily means addiction. Even if someone plays a lot, they may still be choosing to do that. I also do not think gaming is the only available activity. If I do not want to play, I have other things I can do.

**Interviewer:** Do you have any broader thoughts about gaming and why some people may become highly dependent on it?

**Interviewee:** I do not think games themselves are bad. But if we are talking specifically about gaming addiction, one thing I think about is economic accessibility. Games and electronic entertainment are among the cheapest and most accessible forms of leisure. People with fewer financial resources may have fewer alternative forms of entertainment available to them, so games can become a very important low-cost way to relax. That is one reason I think economic circumstances may be relevant when discussing gaming addiction.
