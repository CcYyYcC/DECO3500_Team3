# Interview 5

- **Topic:** FPS Games, Social Gaming, Leisure Choices, Self-Regulation, and Views on Gaming Addiction

## English Transcript

**Interviewer:** First, I need your consent to record this interview. The recording will only be used for our university coursework.

**Interviewee:** Okay, I agree.

**Interviewer:** Do you usually play games? How often do you play, and roughly how much time do you spend gaming?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I play every day. I would say around three to eight hours a day.

**Interviewer:** What type of game do you enjoy most?

**Interviewee:** FPS games.

**Interviewer:** What is the most enjoyable part of an FPS game for you?

**Interviewee:** Probably the kill feedback.

**Interviewer:** Why is kill feedback so satisfying to you?

**Interviewee:** I think it is related to the nature of FPS games. When you defeat an enemy player, that already gives you a satisfying feeling. Then when the game gives you strong feedback for the kill, like sound, visual effects, or other signals, it reinforces that feeling and makes the moment more rewarding.

**Interviewer:** Would the same thing happen if you were just shooting bots or targets?

**Interviewee:** Not really. The important part is that I am playing against another person. If I defeat another player, that feels different. There is more satisfaction because you know there is another real person on the other side.

**Interviewer:** So if we take games like *Valorant* or *Counter-Strike*, is the satisfaction mainly the final kill itself, or also the process leading up to it?

**Interviewee:** The process matters too. Things like tactics, game knowledge, team coordination, positioning, and utility usage are all important because they create the situation where the kill becomes possible. So if someone says the enjoyable part is not just the kill itself, but the whole process of gathering information, making decisions, coordinating with teammates, and then finally getting the kill, I agree with that. I just describe the kill feedback as the most obvious final reward.

**Interviewer:** Do you prefer playing with friends or playing alone?

**Interviewee:** Definitely with friends.

**Interviewer:** What is different about playing with friends compared with random players?

**Interviewee:** For me, gaming has gradually become a social activity. If I am playing with people I already know, I feel more relaxed and comfortable. Playing with strangers is more unpredictable socially. With friends, I do not need to worry as much about how they will react.

**Interviewer:** What makes gaming different from other social situations?

**Interviewee:** In games, the social environment is simpler. In face-to-face social situations, you might think about your appearance, clothing, behaviour, manners, and all kinds of other things. In a game, a lot of that disappears. Your interaction is mainly based around your skill, how well you understand the game, and how you cooperate with other people.

You can help your friends win, support them, or just mess around together. There are fewer things you need to manage socially.

**Interviewer:** If you had to choose between having excellent statistics and rank while playing alone, or playing badly with your friends and losing, which would you choose?

**Interviewee:** I would rather play with my friends. Rank and statistics mostly show how familiar or skilled you are at the game. Even if I had very good statistics, I would still rather play casually with friends and lose than play alone just to maintain a high rank.

**Interviewer:** So would you say the social feedback is more important than the game performance itself?

**Interviewee:** Yes, for me it probably is.

**Interviewer:** But you also sometimes play alone. What motivates you when your friends are not there?

**Interviewee:** If I play alone, it is usually just a way to pass time. For example, if I am already sitting at my computer doing some work and get tired, I might open a game and play for a while.

**Interviewer:** How is that different from using your phone, watching videos, or doing something else to pass time?

**Interviewee:** I do not think there has to be one specific method. It depends on what is convenient at that moment. If I am lying down with my phone, then I might scroll through videos. If I am already at the computer, opening a game is also an easy way to take a break.

**Interviewer:** But playing an FPS can be risky as a way of relaxing. You might meet toxic players, lose badly, or become more frustrated. Why choose that instead of something safer and easier like watching short videos?

**Interviewee:** I think the difference is uncertainty. If I scroll through short videos, I am basically repeating the same action again and again. I swipe, watch something, then swipe again.

In a multiplayer game, even if I play the same map, the opponents are different, the teammates are different, the strategies are different, and my own performance is different. Every match contains many unknown factors.

That uncertainty makes the experience more interesting, even though it also requires more energy.

**Interviewer:** So the unpredictability itself is part of the attraction?

**Interviewee:** Yes. You do not know exactly what is going to happen in each match.

**Interviewer:** If the friends you are playing with decide to stop, do you usually continue alone?

**Interviewee:** Usually I stop too.

**Interviewer:** Has that always been the case?

**Interviewee:** Not necessarily. It depends on the situation and the time of day.

If we are playing in the afternoon and I still have a lot of energy, then if one group stops, I might look for other people to play with. I probably still would not continue completely alone, though.

If it is already late at night and everyone is tired, then when someone says we should stop, I usually stop as well.

**Interviewer:** So your decision to continue depends partly on your own energy and partly on whether there are still people available to play with?

**Interviewee:** Yes.

**Interviewer:** Can you remember a day when you played for a very long time, maybe more than twelve hours?

**Interviewee:** Yes, I have definitely had days like that, although I do not remember one exact day in detail.

**Interviewer:** What usually causes a session like that to become so long?

**Interviewee:** Usually it happens because my friends are also playing. Nobody suggests stopping, we start quite early, and everyone has enough time. Then we just continue.

**Interviewer:** What if you are alone? Would you still play FPS games for that long?

**Interviewee:** Probably not. If I am alone, I usually would not choose something as mentally demanding as *Valorant* or *Counter-Strike* for such a long period. I would play something more relaxed.

**Interviewer:** What kind of relaxed game could keep you playing alone for a long time?

**Interviewee:** *Minecraft* is a good example. I can play that alone because I enjoy building things.

If I start building a house or some other structure, I become curious about what it will look like when it is finished. That expectation can keep me playing for a long time.

**Interviewer:** Is the satisfaction mainly from seeing your own creation completed?

**Interviewee:** Yes, but there can still be a social element. After I build something, I can show it to my friends. If they think it looks good or impressive, that also gives me satisfaction.

**Interviewer:** So even in a single-player activity, social feedback can still matter?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I might build it alone, but part of the reward is being able to show it to other people afterwards.

**Interviewer:** Has gaming ever affected something you originally planned to do?

**Interviewee:** Mostly small things. For example, I might plan to eat dinner at six, but if I am playing, I may keep delaying it.

**Interviewer:** Has it ever affected something more serious, such as an important project, exam, job, or relationship?

**Interviewee:** Not really. For me, gaming usually affects lower-priority plans rather than major responsibilities.

**Interviewer:** How do you prevent it from interfering with important plans?

**Interviewee:** If I know I need to go somewhere, I calculate how long I need to get ready and how long it takes to travel. Then I give myself a latest possible stopping time.

When that time arrives, I stop immediately and leave.

**Interviewer:** Do you use timers, apps, or other external tools?

**Interviewee:** No. It is mostly self-discipline.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever had a period where you deliberately tried to reduce your daily gaming time?

**Interviewee:** Sometimes, but usually it is not because I force myself to reduce it. My interest naturally changes.

For example, if I spend one week playing a lot, then the next week I may feel less interested in games and play much less.

**Interviewer:** So it is more like getting temporarily tired of gaming rather than consciously restricting yourself?

**Interviewee:** Yes, probably. My interest comes in phases.

**Interviewer:** Some people completely stop playing a certain genre after losing interest. You have played FPS games for a long time. Why do you think you keep returning to them?

**Interviewee:** I think the uncertainty is one reason. Every match is still different.

Another reason is familiarity. The longer you play an FPS game, the more familiar you become with aiming, positioning, maps, and game knowledge. Eventually the game feels natural. It does not feel difficult in the way it does when you are a beginner.

**Interviewer:** So familiarity makes the game easier to return to?

**Interviewee:** Yes. At some point it becomes a habit and something you are already comfortable with.

**Interviewer:** If you suddenly stopped gaming, do you think your life would become significantly better?

**Interviewee:** Probably not.

**Interviewer:** Why not?

**Interviewee:** Because if I stop playing games, that does not mean I will automatically use that time to study or exercise.

Imagine that I normally spend ten hours gaming and reduce that to five. The other five hours might just become watching television, watching movies, using my phone, or doing nothing.

So reducing gaming time does not automatically mean that the rest of my life becomes more productive.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever had the feeling that you do not really want to play anymore, but you keep playing simply because you have nothing else to do?

**Interviewee:** Not really. I think there are always other options. I could watch something, use my phone, or do something else. Gaming is one option among several rather than the only thing I can do.

**Interviewer:** Our project is about gaming addiction, so I want to ask how you personally define addiction. Do you think it should be judged by the number of hours someone plays, or more by whether they still have control over their decisions?

**Interviewee:** I think the most important thing is whether it affects the person's intended life.

If someone plays a lot but it does not interfere with the plans and responsibilities they actually care about, I do not necessarily think that is addiction.

**Interviewer:** What about autonomy? For example, someone may no longer want to play, but their friends are still playing and they feel pressured to stay. Or they may not be enjoying the game anymore but still keep playing.

**Interviewee:** I think those things matter, but for me the main question is still whether it affects their real life.

**Interviewer:** So if someone is playing many hours but still feels that they are choosing it and it does not interfere with their responsibilities, you would not automatically call that addiction?

**Interviewee:** Yes.

**Interviewer:** Do you think gaming has had any significant negative effect on your own life?

**Interviewee:** I do not think so. If I played less, I do not think my life would necessarily become very different, because I would probably replace that time with other forms of entertainment.

**Interviewer:** Since you also have a game design background, do you have any broader thoughts about games and gaming addiction?

**Interviewee:** I think games themselves are not a bad thing. They are a form of entertainment, and for many people they are a very accessible one.

But if we are specifically discussing addiction, one thing I think is relevant is economic background.

**Interviewer:** What do you mean?

**Interviewee:** I think electronic entertainment is one of the cheapest forms of leisure available to most people. Once you have the device and the game, you can spend many hours using it without needing to spend much additional money.

Other forms of entertainment can cost much more. Going out, travelling, eating at restaurants, joining certain activities, or doing other hobbies may require more money.

So I think people with fewer financial resources may rely more heavily on games and electronic entertainment because they are among the most affordable ways to relax.

**Interviewer:** So when you think about heavy gaming, you also see it as potentially related to how many alternative leisure activities a person can realistically access?

**Interviewee:** Yes. If someone's available entertainment options are limited, gaming becomes a very easy and low-cost choice.

**Interviewer:** Would you say that this means high gaming time is not always only about the game being exceptionally addictive, but may also be related to the person's wider environment?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I think the environment matters. Gaming is cheap, accessible, and easy to repeat every day, so it can naturally become a major form of entertainment for people who do not have as many other options.

**Interviewer:** So from your perspective, understanding gaming addiction also requires looking beyond game mechanics and considering the person's social and economic situation?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I think that is relevant.
