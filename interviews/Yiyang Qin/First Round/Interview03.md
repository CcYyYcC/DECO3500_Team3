# Interview 3

- **Topic:** Casual Gaming, Social Interaction, Leisure Choices, and Views on Gaming Addiction

## English Transcript

**Interviewer:** First, I need your consent to record this interview. The recording will only be used for our coursework and research activities.

**Interviewee:** I agree.

**Interviewer:** Do you usually play games?

**Interviewee:** Very rarely. I play games only occasionally.

**Interviewer:** When you do play, what kinds of games do you usually choose?

**Interviewee:** Mostly interactive games that involve other people. For example, simple Switch games, party games, cooperative games, or competitive games such as Mario-related games. I generally prefer games that are easy to understand and that I can play together with someone else.

**Interviewer:** So would it be fair to say that you mainly play multiplayer or social games rather than single-player games?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I am much more likely to play something if I can play it with another person. I do not really have a strong habit of playing single-player games by myself.

**Interviewer:** What is the most enjoyable part of gaming for you?

**Interviewee:** The interaction with friends or with my boyfriend. The fun comes from things like competing with each other, cooperating, teasing each other, or deliberately making things difficult for the other person. For me, the game itself is not necessarily the most important thing. The interaction between people is what makes it fun.

**Interviewer:** Can you describe a recent gaming experience in more detail?

**Interviewee:** The last one I remember clearly was when I went to a bathhouse with my boyfriend. We had nothing to do for a while, and there were Switch games available there, so we started playing together. We chose a cooperative game and played for several hours.

He was much better at the game than I was. I kept failing certain parts, so eventually he had to complete them for me. After a while I started feeling unhappy because I felt that if he had to do everything for me, then there was no point in me playing. I was not really participating anymore.

**Interviewer:** So your frustration came from losing the ability to contribute?

**Interviewee:** Yes. If I cannot actually do anything and the other person has to finish everything, then the game stops being meaningful for me. It becomes more like I am watching someone else play.

**Interviewer:** What did you do after that?

**Interviewee:** We changed games and started playing *Overcooked*. That actually caused even more arguments. We both became frustrated and started blaming each other. There was another couple nearby playing the same game, and they were arguing very loudly as well. It looked like they were almost going to fight.

**Interviewer:** What do you think that experience says about cooperative or competitive games?

**Interviewee:** I think people with very strong competitiveness may not always be suited to these kinds of games. Even though the game is supposed to be cooperative or fun, if both people care too much about winning or doing things correctly, it can easily create conflict.

**Interviewer:** Do you also play board games, murder mystery games, or other forms of social games?

**Interviewee:** Very rarely. I have played some, but not often.

**Interviewer:** What do you think is the difference between those activities and video games? Why would you choose a video game?

**Interviewee:** I think video games usually have a lower barrier to entry. They are easier to start, easier to understand, and easier to stop. Something like a murder mystery game has a long setup and usually takes several hours. Once you start, it is difficult to pause halfway through.

With a simple video game, if something suddenly comes up, you can just stop. You can save it or leave it there and continue later. That flexibility makes it easier for me to choose.

**Interviewer:** So being able to stop at any time is important?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I do not like activities where I feel trapped for several hours once I start.

**Interviewer:** If the person you are playing with decides to stop, would you normally continue playing by yourself?

**Interviewee:** Usually no, because the social interaction is the main reason I started playing in the first place. But there are some exceptions.

**Interviewer:** What kind of exceptions?

**Interviewee:** Sometimes I play games that are more focused on the individual experience, such as *Forza Horizon* or *Animal Crossing*. Those games do not depend as much on another person being there.

**Interviewer:** What do you enjoy about those games?

**Interviewee:** In *Forza Horizon*, the main thing I enjoyed was earning money and buying new cars. I liked the process of gradually getting cars that I wanted.

In *Animal Crossing*, I liked things such as dressing the character, changing clothes, decorating, and making things look nice. I think those games can give you a way to experience things that you might not be able to do as easily in real life.

**Interviewer:** So those games are more about personal experience and small forms of progression?

**Interviewee:** Yes. They are more about enjoying the process rather than competing with someone.

**Interviewer:** Has gaming ever affected your original plans, such as study, work, appointments, or sleep?

**Interviewee:** Basically no. I think the main reason is that if I decide to play a game, I usually already know that I have a relatively long and uninterrupted period of free time.

I would not normally be very busy and suddenly decide that I want to start playing. I also would not usually use a small fragmented period of time to start a game. So gaming normally fits into my free time rather than replacing something else I should be doing.

**Interviewer:** So for you, gaming is generally something that happens after you have already decided that the time is free?

**Interviewee:** Yes. It is mainly a way of passing time or entertaining myself when I already have the time available.

**Interviewer:** Are there other hobbies or leisure activities that have affected your plans more strongly than gaming?

**Interviewee:** Drinking probably has.

**Interviewer:** Can you explain that? What makes you choose to drink even if you know it may affect your plans afterwards?

**Interviewee:** Mainly relaxation, stress relief, and entertainment. If I have already been working and dealing with stressful things, drinking makes me feel more relaxed. I stop thinking so much about work and other problems.

Once I am enjoying it, I may stop caring about whatever plans I had originally made. If I am having a good time, I might just think, “I do not care anymore, I will deal with it later.”

**Interviewer:** Has that ever caused conflict with people around you?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I sometimes prefer to go drinking alone, but someone close to me thinks that is unsafe and worries about me. I know that if I tell him, he might disagree or try to stop me, so there have been times when I did not tell him.

That actually made the conflict worse because he became more angry when he found out afterwards.

**Interviewer:** How did you resolve that?

**Interviewee:** Eventually I agreed that I would not go out drinking alone anymore.

**Interviewer:** That is interesting because gaming is something you prefer doing socially, while drinking can be something you prefer doing alone. Why do you think those two forms of entertainment are different for you?

**Interviewee:** I think gaming does not give me enough enjoyment or relaxation by itself. I usually need interaction with another person to make gaming fun. The interaction, competition, teasing, and cooperation are what make it enjoyable.

Drinking is different because the alcohol itself changes how I feel. It can make me relax without needing another person.

There is also another reason. I have had some negative experiences where being drunk around other people affected them emotionally. I may say something inappropriate or cause problems without meaning to. Because of that, sometimes I do not want my drunk state to disturb other people, and I also do not want other people disturbing me. So drinking alone can feel easier.

**Interviewer:** What do you think when you see someone who spends a very large amount of time playing games?

**Interviewee:** I think it is understandable. Different people have different interests and different ways of getting entertainment or relaxation. Some people do not like going outside very much, and they may prefer exploring a virtual world, competing with people, or getting emotional satisfaction through games.

I think those preferences should be respected. Everyone has different ways of enjoying themselves.

**Interviewer:** What about gaming addiction specifically? How do you think it should be understood?

**Interviewee:** I think addiction is different from simply liking games or playing for a long time. Many things can become addictive, including alcohol and other activities. I think you need to look at how much it affects the person's real life.

If someone is completely ignoring study, work, money, responsibilities, and everything else because they only want to play games, then I think that is clearly unhealthy and may require intervention.

**Interviewer:** What if someone plays for a very long time every day but has no responsibilities being neglected? Imagine that they have enough money, someone supports their lifestyle, and they genuinely want to spend most of their time playing games. Would you still call that addiction?

**Interviewee:** In that situation, I think it becomes harder to call it a problem purely because of the number of hours. If their life is not being damaged and they are genuinely choosing to do it, then the amount of time alone may not be enough to say that it is unhealthy.

**Interviewer:** One way I am thinking about addiction is through autonomy. For example, if someone still has control over why they start playing and when they stop, then even a very high amount of gaming may not necessarily mean that they have lost control. But if someone does not actually want to play anymore and still feels compelled to continue, then their autonomy may be lower. What do you think about that distinction?

**Interviewee:** I think that makes sense. Losing control over your own decisions is definitely relevant.

**Interviewer:** For example, imagine someone who only plays a gacha game for two hours a day. Two hours is not extremely long, and it does not seriously damage their work or study. But every day they have to log in and complete daily tasks. They may not enjoy those tasks and may not even want to play that day, but they still feel that they have to do them. Would you consider that a problem?

**Interviewee:** I think it depends. I can understand why you would say that their autonomy is reduced, because they are doing something they do not really want to do.

At the same time, I do not necessarily think that every case like that is a serious problem.

**Interviewer:** Why not?

**Interviewee:** Because if they have a long-term goal, then the repetitive daily activity may simply be part of achieving that goal.

For example, maybe they want to save enough resources to get a character later. They may not enjoy doing the daily tasks today, but they understand that those tasks are necessary if they want to reach the long-term result.

**Interviewer:** So you see the daily task as a cost they voluntarily accept in exchange for a later reward?

**Interviewee:** Yes. It is similar to many other things in life where not every step is enjoyable, but you accept the boring parts because you want the final result.

**Interviewer:** What if the game is deliberately designed so that players lose resources if they do not log in every day?

**Interviewee:** I think that does create pressure, but I would still look at how much it actually affects the person's life. If someone spends a reasonable amount of time on it, still works normally, still studies normally, and is generally happy with what they are doing, I would not immediately say that it is a serious addiction problem.

**Interviewer:** So from your perspective, negative consequences in real life are still more important than whether every individual gaming action feels completely voluntary?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I think the degree of impact matters a lot. You cannot really talk about whether something is good or bad without considering how much of it there is and what consequences it creates.

**Interviewer:** So if someone is spending two hours a day doing repetitive game tasks for a long-term goal, you might see that as acceptable, while someone who completely abandons work and daily responsibilities for games would be a much clearer problem?

**Interviewee:** Yes. The second situation clearly needs intervention. The first one may still involve some pressure or habit, but if it does not significantly interfere with normal life and the person ultimately values the result, I think it is understandable.

**Interviewer:** Would you say that someone can sometimes do something they do not enjoy in the short term while still making an autonomous choice overall?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I think that is possible. If the long-term goal is something they genuinely want, then doing some boring work for it can still be part of their own choice.

**Interviewer:** So for you, gaming addiction cannot be judged only by playtime, and it also cannot necessarily be judged only by whether every moment of play is enjoyable?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I think you have to look at the whole situation: the person's goals, the effect on their life, whether they are still functioning normally, and whether the gaming behaviour has become seriously harmful.
