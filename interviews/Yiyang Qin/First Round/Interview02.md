# Interview 2

- **Topic:** Gacha Games, FPS Games, Narrative Engagement, Habit Formation, and Time Management

## English Transcript

**Interviewer:** First, I need to ask for your consent to record this interview. The recording will only be used for our university coursework.

**Interviewee:** I agree.

**Interviewer:** What types of games do you usually play, and how often do you play them?

**Interviewee:** Mainly gacha games and FPS games. I play games pretty much every day.

**Interviewer:** When you say gacha games, are you referring more to action-based gacha games rather than traditional action games such as *Devil May Cry*?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I have not really played *Devil May Cry*, so I cannot compare them in detail, but I think the focus is different. A game like *Devil May Cry* probably puts more emphasis on combat depth and combos. Gacha games focus much more on characters, progression, collecting, and long-term development.

**Interviewer:** So character appeal is also part of why people play these games?

**Interviewee:** Definitely. For many gacha games, liking the characters is one of the main reasons people start playing. Even in other competitive games, people sometimes choose a character because they like the character design or personality.

**Interviewer:** Can you think of a time when you originally planned to stop playing and do something else, but ended up continuing?

**Interviewee:** That happens quite often. The reason depends on the type of game. If I am playing a gacha game and I am in the middle of a story chapter, I may decide to finish the story before going to eat or doing something else. If I am playing an FPS game, it may be because the last match was not satisfying or because I lost, so I think I will play one more match.

**Interviewer:** What is your thought process when you make that decision?

**Interviewee:** Usually it is something like, “If the thing I need to do is not especially urgent, then I can delay it for a while.” I think my living situation makes that decision easier as well.

**Interviewer:** In what way?

**Interviewee:** When I was studying overseas, I had relatively few classes, and a lot of assignments had long deadlines. So there was often no immediate pressure. That made it easier to think that I could postpone something and continue playing.

**Interviewer:** What kinds of game mechanics are most likely to keep you playing longer than you originally planned?

**Interviewee:** There are many. For gacha games, story content is one. I usually do not like stopping halfway through a story chapter. I prefer to finish a complete section in one sitting, almost like watching a long movie. For FPS games, it is usually competitiveness. If I lose a match, I may want to win one before I stop.

**Interviewer:** Can you give an example of a story that made you continue playing for a long time?

**Interviewee:** A recent story chapter in *Wuthering Waves* was quite good. The story was emotionally engaging, so I played through the whole thing in one sitting. After I finished, I still kept thinking about it and searched online for things like hidden details, interpretations, and story analysis.

**Interviewer:** So the engagement does not necessarily stop when you close the game?

**Interviewee:** Yes. If the story is good enough, I may keep thinking about it afterwards or look for related content online.

**Interviewer:** Does the same thing happen with other narrative-heavy games?

**Interviewee:** Yes. Visual novels are similar. If I start one character route, I often want to finish that entire route before stopping. If I have enough time, I can keep going for a long time.

**Interviewer:** Would you describe that as addiction?

**Interviewee:** I would probably describe it more as immersion. I am continuing because I am interested in the story and want to experience it as a complete narrative.

**Interviewer:** Apart from narrative content, what else makes gacha games effective at keeping players engaged over a long period?

**Interviewee:** Gacha games are very good at building habits. You have daily tasks, resources, stamina, character progression, and things you need to collect regularly. Over time, it can start to feel a bit like clocking in for work. Even if you do not strongly want to play that day, you still think about the resources you will miss if you do not log in.

**Interviewer:** So there is a difference between logging in because you actively want to play and logging in because you feel you should complete the routine?

**Interviewee:** Yes. That is a common design in gacha games. The daily system gives you a reason to return even when the actual desire to play is not very strong.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever experienced conflict with people around you because of gaming?

**Interviewee:** The most common thing is staying up late. My parents used to remind me not to play too late. After I got older, they did not really control me anymore, but they would still tell me to rest earlier.

**Interviewer:** Did you ever change your behaviour because of that?

**Interviewee:** Yes. If you live together, you have to make some compromises. Also, staying up too late is obviously not good for your health. So I would try to adjust my schedule instead of just ignoring them.

**Interviewer:** When you were studying overseas, did other people ever complain about you playing late at night?

**Interviewee:** Not really. I usually played with friends in China, and because of the time difference, a lot of that happened in the afternoon for me. At night, if I was playing alone, I was usually quiet.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever used specific tools to control your gaming time or general screen time?

**Interviewee:** Yes. My computer has reminders that appear every hour and tell me to take a break. I have also used scheduled phone-locking tools. At night, I can set them to block certain apps and only leave a few available, such as QQ or music apps. That way, video apps are unavailable and I am less likely to keep scrolling.

**Interviewer:** Do those tools work consistently?

**Interviewee:** Not always. Sometimes the app gets closed in the background or does not activate properly. If the restriction fails, I may just keep using my phone because the temptation is still there.

**Interviewer:** What happens when that kind of self-control system fails?

**Interviewee:** Usually I stay up too late. Then the next day I either wake up very late or feel tired and have less energy.

**Interviewer:** Do you think that behaviour comes from childhood habits, like the feeling of secretly getting extra screen time when parents are not watching?

**Interviewee:** I do not think so. For me, it is probably more about short-term habits and changes in routine.

**Interviewer:** Can you explain that?

**Interviewee:** For example, if I am playing with friends in China, because of the time difference I may end up eating dinner very late. Then after I eat, I do not want to immediately go to sleep, so I use my phone for a while. Maybe I stay up until four, five, or six in the morning. Then my sleep schedule gets shifted, and once that happens, the pattern can continue for several days.

**Interviewer:** So one unusual late night can create a temporary habit that continues afterwards?

**Interviewee:** Yes. Once the sleep schedule is disrupted, it becomes easier to keep staying up late.

**Interviewer:** Have games ever directly affected your work or study?

**Interviewee:** Yes. Right now I am supposed to be working on a major project, and I am already somewhat behind compared with what I originally planned. I still make time to play games, even though I know I should probably be spending more time on the project.

**Interviewer:** Do you feel guilty about that?

**Interviewee:** Sometimes. I sometimes think that because I am behind, I should completely eliminate entertainment and use all of that time to work. But realistically, I cannot do that. I still need some entertainment.

**Interviewer:** So even when you think you should devote all your time to work, you still keep some gaming time?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I still try to study or work every day as well. Even when I was preparing for IELTS, I was still playing games, but I would make sure I studied for at least two or three hours a day.

**Interviewer:** So you do not usually completely remove gaming. You try to balance it with other responsibilities?

**Interviewee:** Yes. I am not someone who can just stop all entertainment and focus entirely on one thing.

**Interviewer:** Earlier you mentioned the feeling of “one more game” in FPS games. What exactly creates that feeling?

**Interviewee:** Usually it is because the previous match did not feel satisfying. Maybe I lost, or maybe I did not perform well. Then I think I should play another match and stop after getting a better result.

**Interviewer:** Is that similar to the situation where someone keeps playing until they finally win?

**Interviewee:** Yes. It is basically the idea of wanting to end on a good result rather than a bad one.

**Interviewer:** What about gacha games? If we ignore story content for a moment, what other systems keep players returning?

**Interviewee:** The routine systems are probably the biggest thing. Daily tasks, stamina, limited resources, progression materials, and rewards all create a reason to log in. The game gradually builds a routine around the player.

**Interviewer:** Do you think that routine reduces the player's autonomy?

**Interviewee:** It can. If you are logging in because there are resources you feel you cannot waste, then you are no longer only playing because you actively want to play at that moment.

**Interviewer:** Why do you think developers use those systems so heavily?

**Interviewee:** Because live-service games need active users. They need people logging in regularly, interacting with the game, and staying connected to the system. Daily and weekly tasks are a very effective way to maintain that activity.

**Interviewer:** Since you are also interested in game design, do you think these systems are simply manipulative, or do they also have legitimate design purposes?

**Interviewee:** Both. They are clearly used to maintain engagement, but they also give players structure and goals. The problem is more about how strongly the system pressures the player and how much freedom the player feels they still have.

**Interviewer:** Do you think different genres create different kinds of long-term engagement?

**Interviewee:** Definitely. FPS games rely more on competition, skill, uncertainty, and the desire to recover from a loss. Narrative games can rely on immersion and wanting to finish a story. Gacha games rely much more on long-term progression, characters, resource systems, daily routines, and habit formation.

**Interviewer:** Do you have any broader thoughts on FPS games as a genre?

**Interviewee:** I think pure gunfighting by itself can actually become repetitive. A lot of successful FPS games add other systems around the shooting. In *Delta Force*, you collect information and make decisions before a fight. In games like *Overwatch* or *Apex Legends*, there are abilities and team combinations. Those systems make the game more interesting because success is not only determined by raw aiming ability.

**Interviewer:** Why is that important?

**Interviewee:** Because shooting itself can be exhausting and frustrating. If the entire experience depends only on aim, then mistakes can feel very punishing. Adding information, abilities, positioning, tactics, and teamwork gives players other ways to contribute and reduces how much everything depends on mechanical skill.

**Interviewer:** But games like *Counter-Strike* have remained successful for a very long time while putting a lot of emphasis on competition and mechanical mastery. How do you see that?

**Interviewee:** I think there are two broad directions: competitive design and entertainment-focused design. A highly competitive game can stay alive for a long time because players continue chasing rank, mastery, and improvement, similar to traditional competitive activities. Other games try to mix competitive elements with more entertaining or varied mechanics.

**Interviewer:** So developers often try to find a balance between pure competition and entertainment?

**Interviewee:** Yes. Some games add entertainment systems to a competitive structure, while others add competitive systems to something more casual. Whether that works depends on how well the game understands what its core experience is.

**Interviewer:** Do you think failed games sometimes misunderstand that balance?

**Interviewee:** Yes. Sometimes a game has an interesting concept or a fun initial experience, but that idea is not enough to support a long-term game by itself. Players may enjoy a short demo or early version, but the developers still need to build enough depth, progression, content, and support around it. Good initial feedback does not automatically mean the game will work as a long-term live product.

**Interviewer:** So from your perspective, the thing that keeps a player engaged is rarely just one mechanic?

**Interviewee:** Yes. It is usually a combination of things: the core gameplay, progression, social interaction, story, routine, and long-term goals. Different games rely on different combinations.
