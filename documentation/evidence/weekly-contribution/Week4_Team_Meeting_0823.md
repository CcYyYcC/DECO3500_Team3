# DECO3500 Week 4 Team Meeting

**Date:** 23 August 2026  
**Meeting length:** Approximately 50 minutes  
**Main purpose:** Review the stories/design opportunities developed from the interview analysis, agree on a clearer project direction, and discuss possible interaction and social features for the next stage.

## 1. Main Discussion

### 1.1 Do not treat gaming itself as the problem
The team agreed that the interview findings do not support the simple assumption that gaming is inherently negative. For many participants, gaming is an enjoyable and effective form of relaxation. It can satisfy several needs at once, including entertainment, story, visual stimulation, achievement, feedback, and social interaction.

The issue is therefore not simply **how long people play games**, but how people manage gaming alongside other responsibilities such as study, work, exercise, relationships, and deadlines.

### 1.2 Time restriction does not solve the underlying issue
The team discussed how hard restrictions, such as time locks or traditional anti-addiction systems, may reduce gaming time temporarily but do not necessarily reduce the desire to play. In some cases, strong restrictions may even create resistance or make gaming feel more attractive because it becomes something that is forbidden.

The interviews suggested that people are more likely to reduce gaming when they personally recognise that another activity or responsibility has become more important.

### 1.3 Autonomy and prioritisation became the key direction
After comparing several stories, the discussion gradually converged on **user autonomy and prioritisation**.

The team agreed that the design should not tell users that gaming is a “bad choice” or force them to stop. Instead, it should help users:

- recognise what currently needs their attention;
- understand the relative priority of different activities;
- make their own decisions about how to allocate time;
- become more aware of the consequences of delaying important tasks.

The wording **“prioritise”** was preferred over **“make a better choice”**, because the project should avoid implying that gaming is automatically worse than study, work, or other activities.

## 2. Stories / Opportunities Discussed

### Story 2 — Sustained engagement and time allocation
One early idea was to visualise how much attention a user gives to different parts of life, such as gaming, study, exercise, and rest. Possible metaphors included progress bars, racing objects, rockets, or other physical indicators.

This direction was considered useful because it makes time allocation visible without directly banning gaming.

### Story 5 — Gaming as escape from real-life frustration
The team discussed participants who use games as a safe space when they feel frustrated, overwhelmed, or avoidant about real-life tasks.

A possible opportunity was to support people in facing difficult real-life responsibilities, potentially with friends or peers who are also trying to make progress. This introduced ideas around companionship, shared progress, and positive social motivation.

However, the team felt this direction could become too psychological or difficult to translate into a clear interaction design within the project scope.

### Story 1 — Limits of controlling gaming time
This story received strong support because it offered several possible design entry points. The main insight was:

> Reducing gaming time does not necessarily solve the underlying problem.

The team connected this to autonomy and priority awareness: people often stop or reduce gaming when other responsibilities become personally meaningful or urgent, rather than because an external system forces them to stop.

This became the strongest foundation for the current direction.

## 3. Current Design Opportunity

The working design opportunity can be summarised as:

**How might we support players in recognising and prioritising important real-life responsibilities, so they can autonomously manage their attention and time without treating gaming itself as a problem?**

The intended experience is a **soft intervention**, rather than a hard restriction. The system should increase awareness and support self-directed decision-making.

## 4. Emerging Prototype Concept — Gamified Deadline Visualisation

The team developed a concept around making deadlines and responsibilities visually present.

### Core metaphor
- The user is represented by a moving character, such as a surfer.
- Real-life tasks or deadlines are represented by sharks, monsters, or other pursuing threats.
- As a deadline approaches, the corresponding threat moves closer.
- When the user makes progress on the task, the threat can be pushed back.
- When the task is completed, the threat can disappear or be defeated.
- Multiple tasks could be represented by multiple sharks or different types of monsters.

The purpose is not to punish gaming. A user can still spend time gaming if they choose. The important outcome is that they remain aware of their responsibilities and successfully manage them before the “sharks” catch up.

The team liked this concept because it turns an abstract deadline into a more immediate and playful interaction while still preserving user autonomy.

## 5. Social Interaction Discussion

The team discussed that the course definition of **social** may be broader than direct person-to-person communication. The design may be able to satisfy social qualities through awareness, shared activity, collaboration, or multiplayer-related elements.

Possible directions discussed included:

- seeing that a friend is also making progress;
- linking two physical devices;
- visualising the progress of friends or housemates;
- working toward similar deadlines together;
- using gentle comparison or positive competition;
- creating a sense that another person is also “there” and working alongside you.

The team agreed that the final concept does **not necessarily need a traditional leaderboard or full multiplayer system**. However, the exact social requirement still needs to be checked carefully against the course criteria.

## 6. Key Decisions

1. **Gaming itself will not be framed as the problem.**
2. **The project will move away from hard gaming-time restrictions.**
3. **The key design focus is user autonomy, awareness, and prioritisation.**
4. **The current preferred concept is a gamified visualisation of real-life tasks/deadlines.**
5. **The “shark/deadline” metaphor is a promising concept for further development.**
6. **Social features should support awareness, companionship, collaboration, or shared progress rather than being added only for competition.**
7. The concept still needs to be narrowed and developed into a clear interactive experience.

## 7. Open Questions

- How should task progress be entered or detected?
- What interaction should users perform to show progress?
- Should the prototype be primarily physical, digital, or a combination?
- How can the concept avoid becoming a standard productivity app or Pomodoro timer?
- Which visual metaphor is most effective: sharks, monsters, racing objects, or another form?
- How should social awareness or collaboration be incorporated?
- Which specific course “social” criteria must the prototype demonstrate?
- How can the team evaluate whether the design actually increases awareness and supports autonomous prioritisation?
