## 1. Slow Technology

### Definition

Slow technology is a design approach that uses time, gradual change, or deliberate friction to encourage reflection and mental rest rather than maximising speed and efficiency. It allows meaning and understanding to develop through long-term interaction.

**Key ideas:** Time · Reflection · Gradual understanding

### Example: SoundMirror ("Flashbacks")

SoundMirror uses microphones to record short sound fragments in an office corridor and replay them through loudspeakers after different time delays. As people repeatedly hear sounds from the recent past, they gradually recognise patterns connecting past and present activities.

It is an example of slow technology because its meaning is not delivered immediately. Understanding develops over time and encourages people to pay attention to their surroundings and reflect on the corridor as a shared public space.

### Relevance to Our Project

Slow technology suggests that our design should not simply block gaming or demand an immediate behavioural change. The Time Bank could use gradually changing LED feedback to make the relationship between gaming time and other priorities visible. This gives users time to reflect and decide what deserves attention while preserving their autonomy.

## 2. Tangible Interaction

### Definition

Tangible interaction allows users to interact with digital information through physical objects, surfaces, or movements. It connects digital “bits” with physical “atoms,” making interaction more direct and embodied.

**Key ideas:** Physical manipulation · Embodied interaction · Digital–physical connection

### Example: Tangible Control Cube

A tangible control cube can trigger different digital functions when a user flips, rotates, or moves it. The orientation and movement of the physical object become inputs to the digital system. This replaces abstract menus with actions that users can see, touch, and remember.

### Why Is It Different?

Instead of interacting only through a screen, tangible interaction uses familiar physical actions such as holding, moving, flipping, or stacking objects. Physical form can make digital information easier to understand because location, shape, colour, weight, and movement can all communicate meaning.

Embodied interaction may also support memory and awareness. Users can connect information with the location and state of a physical object rather than remembering information shown only on a screen. The direct relationship between action and feedback can make an interaction more intuitive and meaningful.

### Design Outcome: Tangible Task Blocks

Each physical block represents a digital task or category of time. Users can move a block between areas such as **Later**, **Today**, and **Done**. An approaching deadline can be communicated through subtle light or vibration.

This direction is closely related to the Time Bank concept. The physical modules allow users to start, stop, store, compare, and prioritise different categories of time through direct manipulation rather than a conventional task-management interface.

## 3. Speculative Design

### Definition

Speculative design uses plausible future or alternative scenarios to question current assumptions, values, and technologies. Rather than solving a problem directly, it creates artefacts or situations that provoke reflection and discussion about what could or should happen.

**Key ideas:** “What if?” · Possible futures · Provocation · Debate

### Example: Forget Me Not Frame

Forget Me Not Frame is a speculative digital photo frame in which an image gradually fades over time. A person must actively interact with the frame to preserve or restore the memory.

The concept is not intended to be a normal consumer product. Instead, it uses an imagined technology to raise questions about memory, emotional attachment, family relationships, and whether technology should preserve personal memories for us.

### Difference from Regular Design

**Regular design:** Problem → Solution → Improved usability  
**Speculative design:** Assumption → Imagined future → Reaction → Reflection on values

Technology acts as a provocation or thought experiment. It makes a possible future concrete enough for people to react to, question, and debate. The value of the design therefore comes from the discussion it creates rather than its immediate usefulness.

### Design Outcome: Responsibility Score

We imagined a future gaming platform that calculates a player's real-life **Responsibility Score** using deadlines and activity data. A low score could become visible inside the game or to friends and could affect the player's gaming experience.

This is deliberately uncomfortable. It raises questions such as:

- Should a gaming platform monitor a player's real-life responsibilities?
- Who decides what counts as responsible behaviour?
- Should personal productivity data affect leisure and social status?
- When does behavioural support become surveillance or judgement?

The purpose is not to propose this system as a final solution. It helps the team recognise ethical boundaries and reinforces the importance of autonomy, privacy, and user-controlled prioritisation in our actual design.

## Overall Learning

The three concepts provide different perspectives on our physical time-management project:

- **Slow Technology** shows how gradual feedback can create time for reflection.
- **Tangible Interaction** shows how physical objects can make digital time and priorities visible and directly manipulable.
- **Speculative Design** helps us question the possible social and ethical consequences of monitoring or influencing gaming behaviour.

Together, they suggest that the Time Bank should be a tangible and reflective tool rather than a system that controls, judges, or publicly ranks users. Its purpose should be to support awareness and personal decision-making while respecting user autonomy.
