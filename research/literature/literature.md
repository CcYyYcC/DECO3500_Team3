# Literature Research

*Evidence Behind Each Design Iteration — DECO3500 Team 3*

This document maps four highly relevant papers to each of the three design iteration stages described in Design_Iteration_Process.docx. For each paper: what it found, the insight the team drew from it, and its implication for a specific decision in the current project. Read stage-by-stage, the sequence is intended to be logically coherent — each stage's papers justify why the team moved from the previous solution to the next.

## Stage 1 — Research & Reframing: Why We Rejected a “Blocker”

**Ryan, R. M., Rigby, C. S., & Przybylski, A. (2006). The motivational pull of video games: A self-determination theory approach. Motivation and Emotion, 30(4), 347–363.**

**Summary:** Using Self-Determination Theory (SDT), the study found that perceived in-game autonomy and competence predict enjoyment, continued play, and short-term well-being changes — play is driven by genuine psychological need satisfaction, not simply compulsion.

**Insight:** Gaming itself is not inherently harmful; it satisfies real autonomy, competence and relatedness needs. This directly supports Story 1 and Story 5's finding that identical play time can reflect a healthy, chosen source of satisfaction.

**Implication for the project:** Justified the team's first key decision — not to treat gaming as a problem to be minimised — and to design around preserving player autonomy rather than removing choice through restriction.

**Kardefelt-Winther, D. (2014). A conceptual and methodological critique of internet addiction research: Towards a model of compensatory internet use. Computers in Human Behavior, 31, 351–354.**

**Summary:** Proposes that much “excessive” internet/gaming use is compensatory — a coping response to unmet offline psychosocial needs — rather than a primary addiction in its own right.

**Insight:** Matches Story 5 precisely: the same high play time can be autonomous interest investment for one participant and compensation for low achievement/confidence for another. The behaviour cannot be judged without the underlying reason.

**Implication for the project:** Motivated a design that helps surface why a player is choosing to play right now (task-priority comparison), rather than one that only measures or caps how long they play.

**Billieux, J., Schimmenti, A., Khazaal, Y., Maurage, P., & Heeren, A. (2015). Are we overpathologizing everyday life? A tenable blueprint for behavioral addiction research. Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 4(3), 119–123.**

**Summary:** Argues that behavioural addiction research often mislabels normal high engagement as pathology through atheoretical, symptom-count screening instruments.

**Insight:** Validated the team's discomfort with a deficit/addiction framing of participants who were simply enthusiastic, high-engagement players rather than clinically impaired.

**Implication for the project:** Reinforced the decision to reject a restriction/blocking tool (which assumes pathology) in favour of a supportive, prioritisation-based framing that respects everyday high engagement as normal.

**Przybylski, A. K., Weinstein, N., & Murayama, K. (2017). Internet gaming disorder: Investigating the clinical relevance of a new phenomenon. American Journal of Psychiatry, 174(3), 230–236.**

**Summary:** A large-scale study found that proposed Internet Gaming Disorder criteria correlated strongly with game engagement but only weakly and inconsistently with real-world physical, social and mental health outcomes — games appeared meaningfully less addictive than gambling.

**Insight:** Reduces the likelihood that the team's target users are clinically addicted; the more common issue is everyday mis-prioritisation in the moment, not disorder.

**Implication for the project:** Legitimised scoping the product toward everyday time-prioritisation support rather than a clinical-intensity intervention, keeping the design proportionate to the actual problem observed in interviews.

## Stage 2 — Ideation: From Competitive Gamification to Ambient Social Awareness

**Deterding, S., Dixon, D., Khaled, R., & Nacke, L. (2011). From game design elements to gamefulness: Defining “gamification”. Proceedings of the 15th International Academic MindTrek Conference, 9–15.**

**Summary:** Defines gamification as the use of game design elements in non-game contexts, and distinguishes it from related concepts such as serious games and playful design.

**Insight:** Gave the team precise vocabulary to name and later critique the initial MOBA-ranking brainstorm and Solution 1's RPG framing as a textbook gamification approach — borrowing game elements (roles, battles, rankings) wholesale.

**Implication for the project:** Enabled a structured review of Solution 1, revealing it leaned on surface game elements (points, battles) rather than the deeper motivational mechanisms identified in Stage 1.

**Sailer, M., Hense, J. U., Mayr, S. K., & Mandl, H. (2017). How gamification motivates: An experimental study of the effects of specific game design elements on psychological need satisfaction. Computers in Human Behavior, 69, 371–380.**

**Summary:** Experimentally shows that different game elements satisfy different psychological needs unevenly — badges and performance graphs support competence, while leaderboard-style competitive elements can undermine autonomy for some users.

**Insight:** Explains, mechanistically, why Solution 1's “defeat the dragon” win/lose framing risked undermining the autonomy that Stage 1 identified as essential — competitive elements are not universally motivating.

**Implication for the project:** Directly justified pivoting away from competitive/win-lose mechanics toward design elements that support relatedness and awareness instead, shaping Solution 2.

**Dourish, P., & Bellotti, V. (1992). Awareness and coordination in shared workspaces. Proceedings of CSCW '92, 107–114.**

**Summary:** Introduces “awareness” in CSCW: passively perceiving others' activity in a shared space supports loose coordination without requiring direct communication or comparison.

**Insight:** Provided the theoretical grounding for Solution 2's “Shared Rhythm” panel — simply seeing others' timelines can build social awareness without demanding interaction, ranking, or comparison.

**Implication for the project:** Directly shaped Solution 2's explicit “different rhythms, shared awareness — companionship, not competition” framing, and carried forward into the multi-tower display of Solution 3.

**Ishii, H., & Ullmer, B. (1997). Tangible bits: Towards seamless interfaces between people, bits and atoms. Proceedings of CHI '97, 234–241.**

**Summary:** Proposes Tangible User Interfaces (TUIs): coupling digital information to graspable physical objects, and using ambient media (light, sound, movement) to represent information at the periphery of attention.

**Insight:** Gave the team a design language for turning an abstract concept — a daily schedule — into something physically manipulable, rather than a screen-based planner or app.

**Implication for the project:** Directly motivated the shift from a purely gamified device (Solution 1) to a block-based tangible artefact (Solution 2), and carried through into the physical, flip-to-start tokens of Solution 3.

## Stage 3 — Convergence: Justifying the Current Prototype

**Pousman, Z., & Stasko, J. (2006). A taxonomy of ambient information systems: Four patterns of design. Proceedings of AVI 2006, 67–74.**

**Summary:** Provides a design vocabulary for ambient information systems — including abstract, symbolic representation of data intended to be glanced at rather than read in detail.

**Insight:** The Time Sand Tower's colour- and shape-coded accumulation of sand is a textbook ambient display pattern, not a literal data dashboard — it communicates a day's shape at a glance rather than exact figures.

**Implication for the project:** Validated designing the Sand Tower as an ambient, glanceable artefact instead of a numeric time-tracking screen, keeping it low-pressure and consistent with Stage 1's autonomy-preserving intent.

**Consolvo, S., McDonald, D. W., Toscos, T., Chen, M. Y., Froehlich, J., Harrison, B., Klasnja, P., LaMarca, A., LeGrand, L., Libby, R., Smith, I., & Landay, J. A. (2008). Activity sensing in the wild: A field trial of UbiFit Garden. Proceedings of CHI 2008, 1797–1806.**

**Summary:** UbiFit Garden used an ambient, gently gamified mobile display — a garden that blooms with physical activity — to motivate behaviour change without explicit numeric tracking, scores, or shaming.

**Insight:** Demonstrates that ambient, aesthetic feedback sustains motivation respectfully, in contrast to Solution 1's explicit win/lose battle mechanic.

**Implication for the project:** Directly informed making the Sand Tower an aesthetic, hourglass-like artefact rather than a leaderboard or streak counter, consistent with the Stage 2 pivot away from competitive framing.

**Gollwitzer, P. M. (1999). Implementation intentions: Strong effects of simple plans. American Psychologist, 54(7), 493–503.**

**Summary:** Shows that pre-committing to concrete “if situation X, then response Y” plans (implementation intentions) substantially improves follow-through compared to vague goals or willpower alone.

**Insight:** Explains why the Time Token's flip-to-start action works as a behaviour-change mechanism: it is a concrete, situational commitment (“flip = I am now doing X”), not an abstract resolution to “play less.”

**Implication for the project:** Grounded the token's simple physical flip-to-commit interaction as the core mechanism for the moment-of-choice prioritisation identified as the design opportunity in Stage 1.

**Lin, J. J., Mamykina, L., Lindtner, S., Delajoux, G., & Strub, H. B. (2006). Fish'n'Steps: Encouraging physical activity with an interactive computer game. Proceedings of UbiComp 2006, 261–278.**

**Summary:** Linking step count to a growing virtual fish, shared among players, sustained engagement over a 14-week trial and created cooperative (and mildly competitive) social motivation, though enthusiasm faded once new routines were established.

**Insight:** Confirms a shared, ambient visual representation (their fish; the team's sand tower) can sustain behaviour change, but also warns that purely comparative shared displays carry a competitive risk if not carefully framed, and that novelty fade is a realistic long-term limitation.

**Implication for the project:** Reinforced keeping the Sand Tower's multi-tower, end-of-day comparison framed as companionship (per Dourish & Bellotti, Stage 2) rather than ranking, and flagged novelty fade as a limitation to watch for in user evaluation.

## Summary

Across all three stages, the literature consistently points in one direction: gaming behaviour should not be judged or restricted by time alone (Stage 1), motivation is best sustained through autonomy-preserving and ambient — not competitive — design elements (Stage 2), and concrete, situational commitment devices combined with ambient, aesthetic feedback are what make behaviour-change prototypes both effective and buildable (Stage 3). This is the throughline connecting the initial interviews to the current Time Token + Time Sand Tower prototype.
