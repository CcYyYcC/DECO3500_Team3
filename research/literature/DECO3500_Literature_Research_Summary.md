# Literature Research: From Gaming Addiction to Tangible Time Awareness

## Overview

Our assigned domain was *Combatting Computer Game Addiction*. We began by reviewing why digital games encourage continued participation and why stopping can be difficult. The early research covered reinforcement and rewards, limited-time tasks, sunk costs, and social obligations. However, the literature also challenged the assumption that long gaming sessions are necessarily problematic. We therefore moved from asking how to **reduce gaming time** towards asking how a design might support **autonomy, priority awareness, and deliberate choices**. Our literature review developed through three connected stages.

## Stage 1 — Understanding Gaming Behaviour and Reframing the Problem

Ryan, Rigby, and Przybylski (2006) used Self-Determination Theory to explain how games can meet psychological needs such as autonomy and competence. This helped us avoid describing gaming only as a harmful habit. Kardefelt-Winther (2014) proposed compensatory internet use as a way to understand how online activity may respond to offline difficulties or unmet needs. Billieux et al. (2015) questioned the tendency to label highly engaged everyday behaviour as addiction, while Przybylski, Weinstein, and Murayama (2017) examined whether proposed gaming-disorder indicators meaningfully correspond with real-life impairment.

Together, these sources suggested that **time spent gaming is not sufficient on its own to interpret a person's experience**. Motivation, context, perceived control, and consequences also matter. We used this perspective to refine our interview direction, moving beyond the original focus on MOBA players and asking more open questions about gaming, other responsibilities, and moments of conflict. Our later interview synthesis—particularly refined Stories 1 and 5—supported a design opportunity focused on recognising priorities rather than imposing universal time limits. The interview findings are separate evidence from the literature itself.

## Stage 2 — Moving Beyond Competitive Gamification

Our early ideas included game-like pressure, deadlines presented as threats, and achievement-based progression. Deterding et al. (2011) helped us describe these features more precisely as *gamification*. Sailer et al. (2017) showed that different game elements relate to psychological needs in different ways; we took this as a reason **not to assume competitive mechanics would motivate every user**. Our concern that threat-based concepts might increase stress was a team design judgement, not a direct finding of those two papers.

We then considered a less competitive social experience. Dourish and Bellotti (1992) introduced awareness as an important resource for coordination in shared environments. This informed our idea of making activity records visible to others without requiring rankings or direct messaging. Ishii and Ullmer (1997) provided a foundation for *tangible interaction*, helping us rethink time as information that could be handled physically rather than entered into another screen-based planner. These ideas supported the shift through Physical Landscape toward separate Time Tokens and a shared Time Tower.

## Stage 3 — Ambient Feedback, Physical Commitment, and Shared Reflection

Pousman and Stasko (2006) provided design principles for ambient information displays, suggesting how information might remain visible at the edge of attention. Consolvo et al. (2008), through UbiFit Garden, offered a relevant example of representing activity through a visual form rather than only numerical statistics. Lin et al. (2006), through Fish'n'Steps, provided another precedent for connecting personal activity records with a social visualisation, while also reminding us to consider competition and long-term engagement.

Gollwitzer's (1999) work on *implementation intentions* helped us think about the value of a clear, intentional starting action. We drew an analogy to flipping a Token to begin a chosen activity; **the paper does not prove that our flip gesture will improve behaviour**. Slow Technology (Hallnäs & Redström, 2001), explored in our design-framework activity, also influenced the preference for gradual, ambient LED feedback instead of constant correction.

The resulting system uses three activity Tokens (Study, Exercise, Gaming), a deliberate flip-to-start interaction, and a Time Tower that accumulates completed time units. The aim is to offer a visible record that may support individual and shared reflection, without scoring people as productive or unproductive.

## Research-to-Design Synthesis

| Literature-informed consideration | Design response | Remaining question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High engagement does not by itself establish harm or disorder | Do not diagnose addiction or automatically restrict gaming | Can users interpret their own priorities rather than just the duration displayed? |
| Motivation and autonomy are important | Keep activity choice with the user and avoid punishment | Is the hourly restart helpful or disruptive during real work? |
| Game elements do not motivate all people in the same way | Move away from threat, battle and leaderboard concepts | Does a non-competitive presentation feel less judgemental? |
| Ambient information can make patterns visible without constant screen interaction | Use LED progress and accumulated activity blocks | Are the colours, symbols and time units understandable at a glance? |
| Awareness of others can support interaction but can also invite comparison | Present separate shared activity traces without ranking users | What information are housemates actually willing to share? |
| Concrete actions can support commitment | Use a deliberate physical flip as the start of a time unit | Does this action create useful reflection over time? |

## Critical Reflection and Evidence Limits

The literature gave us **design rationales and precedents**, not proof that our particular device changes habits. Studies of physical activity displays cannot automatically establish an effect on gaming or study decisions. Similarly, the CSCW literature supports exploring shared awareness but does not show that a public time display will improve relationships between housemates. Our formative prototype evaluation considered usability and reactions to simulated shared records; it did not test long-term behaviour change or natural paired use. Future research should examine actual hour-long sessions, shared-space interaction, voluntary disclosure and the possibility of unwanted social pressure.
