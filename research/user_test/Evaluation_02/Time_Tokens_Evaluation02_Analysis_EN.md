## Executive summary

Using cleaned P01/P02 transcripts from **eight-second-per-hour demonstrations** with a programmed housemate B, this analysis examines how people interpret shared time records and the Token interaction. It is a small, prompted, partly role-played corpus—not an evaluation of natural multi-user use.

After prompting, participants could interpret completed activity units, but some **activity symbols, record ownership, time semantics and device states** remained unclear. Staged comparisons raised possible social pressure; excessive brightness and cable-dependent portability constrained the experience. Next priorities are **clear feedback, interpretable and privacy-sensitive sharing, and real paired/contextual evaluation**.

## 1. Evaluation context and research questions

**System and scenario.** The prototype presented three activity categories (Exercise, Study and Gaming), a one-hour time unit represented by an accelerated **8-second** demo, completed-time visualisations on a shared Tower, and an additional record stream for simulated housemate B. Participants were asked to perform activities, interpret the Tower and imagine a housemate interaction. This configuration evaluates comprehension and reactions to an **enacted sharing scenario**; it cannot by itself establish genuine household interaction or long-term habit change.

**Research questions (analytical framing; not claims of demonstrated success):**

- **RQ1 — Representation:** How do people interpret ownership, activity categories, completed time units and the order of records?
- **RQ2 — Social experience:** What comparisons, conversations or disclosure boundaries arise when individual records are presented as shared information?
- **RQ3 — Interaction:** Where does the Token's start, restart and transfer-feedback language create uncertainty?
- **RQ4 — Mobile/contextual use:** How do activity location, movement, visibility and hardware constraints shape use?
- **RQ5 — Design iteration:** Which findings justify a change, and which are unresolved questions requiring another evaluation?

**Method alignment.** Week 8's evaluation teaching calls for formative inquiry, social interaction and evaluation in context, organised around **design objective → evaluation questions → evidence** (DECO3500, Week 8 Evaluation slides, pp. 3–4 and 7). The present analysis uses that logic without treating these prompted scenarios as a substitute for real paired observation.

## 2. Data preparation and analytic method

1. **Corpus inspection.** Read P01 and P02 in their original sequence; retain numbered segments such as `P01-017` and `P02-044` as evidence locators. Do not turn the cleaned transcripts into invented verbatim audio quotes.
2. **Evidence-status classification.** Mark whether a segment is (a) a named P01 utterance, (b) researcher prompt or explanation, (c) staged dialogue with uncertain attribution, (d) retrospective report, or (e) hypothetical future preference. These are not interchangeable kinds of evidence.
3. **Initial coding.** Apply descriptive codes to statements about visual understanding, user attribution, comparison, discretion, time granularity, feedback and context.
4. **Theme formation.** Group related codes into six themes, checking for tensions rather than forcing all comments into positive or negative findings.
5. **Design synthesis.** Pair each theme with a specific implication and status: **retained decision**, **proposed improvement**, or **requires further testing**.

The transcripts explicitly identify themselves as **lightly cleaned, not unabridged verbatim**. They also warn that portions of staged discussion contain multiple unseparated voices; attribution of some remarks to P02 or to a specific participant would be unjustified. Only statements explicitly tagged `P01:` are attributed directly to P01 in this report. Quotations from unattributed dialogue are labelled as such. For publication as a direct quotation, check the original recording and the speaker assignment first.

## 3. Thematic findings

### T1 — Activity accumulation can be understood, but the trace is not a clock-synchronised diary

**Evidence.** After a researcher asked P01 to visit the shared display, P01 identified the two pink gaming symbols as the two periods just recorded (`P01-015`, `P01-017`). P01 also interpreted most of another column's ten recorded units, but required the researcher to identify an unfamiliar activity symbol (`P01-019`–`022`). In a later interview response, P01 said that quantities, proportions and sequence were the main information used to make judgments (`P01-040`). A staged, unattributed dialogue warned that separate users' columns need not indicate what each person was doing at the same moment (`P01-026`, `P01-032`).

**Interpretation.** The accumulating-block format provides an accessible starting point for **recorded duration and within-column order**, particularly once the user is directed to it. However, it has semantic boundaries: it is not necessarily a complete record of someone's day, a real-time availability signal or a shared clock-time schedule. The scenario raised this concern, but it was not independently verified as a widespread misunderstanding.

**Design implication — proposed.** Add a concise on-display or nearby legend: **one pattern = one completed hour; order = submission/recorded sequence; the two users' columns do not imply simultaneous activities**. Define whether the system represents completion order or activity-start order before claiming either. Distinguish the simulated one-hour demonstration from actual elapsed time.

### T2 — Shared records invite social interpretation, but can also invite unwanted evaluation

**Evidence.** In a direct interview answer, P01 expressed willingness to share because exact start times were absent (`P01-042`). The prompted, staged P02 discussion included comparisons between mostly gaming-based and mixed-activity records, suggestions about what another person should do, and self-evaluative remarks (`P02-012`–`023`). After being asked about feelings, one unidentified voice said reversed records might cause anxiety; another said they would not be particularly affected (`P02-026`–`027`). These are **not reliably attributable to P02** and are not unprompted, real-housemate interaction.

**Interpretation.** The Tower has the potential to turn private activity logs into **socially discussable objects**. Yet visibility is double-edged: it may support curiosity or conversation, while inviting normative judgments about how time *should* be spent. Different responses in the same staged discussion also caution against assuming all people welcome comparison.

**Design implication — proposed.** Position shared data as **recorded activity, not a productivity score**. Do not introduce rankings or moral labels. Investigate who can see records, whether a participant may choose not to submit, and how privacy expectations vary across Study, Gaming and Exercise. Current transcripts do not establish that such controls are implemented.

### T3 — Attribution, activity symbols and display comfort require clearer visual communication

**Evidence.** An unattributed P02 discussion contributor asked how viewers know which column belongs to which person, suggested labels, and noted that anonymity may make near-identical columns harder to recognise (`P02-036`, `P02-038`, `P02-040`). Another comment questioned the two-column limit (`P02-031`). Participants in the staged discussion associated green with an hourglass, red with a cross or warning, and another green symbol with “Z”, showing that visual interpretations differ (`P02-048`, `P02-051`). Individual customisation of shapes/colours was questioned because it might weaken comparison (`P02-053`). Brightness was described as uncomfortable for sustained viewing (`P02-062`, `P02-064`). P01's direct uncertainty about a symbol independently supports the need for a legend (`P01-021`).

**Interpretation.** A record may be technically displayed but still fail to be **readable, interpretable or comfortable to examine**. Visibility therefore depends on attribution cues, a stable activity code and suitable display intensity—not simply on whether an LED lights up.

**Design implication — proposed.** Test non-identifying column markers (e.g., A/B or participant-selected labels) before publishing real names; add a persistent activity legend; use a shared coding scheme; adjust brightness for a typical shared room; record the current two-column capacity as a prototype limitation. Do not present user-controlled naming or brightness adjustment as finished unless implemented and tested.

### T4 — The one-hour checkpoint is a deliberate design choice, while the unit size leaves some activity unrepresented

**Evidence.** An unattributed P02 discussion questioned what happens when an activity lasts **one and a half hours** but only a full hour produces a block (`P02-044`); smaller increments were suggested (`P02-046`). Another voice requested actual clock-time markers for comparing a day against a plan (`P02-041`, `P02-043`). By contrast, P01 directly valued the current absence of exact start times when considering disclosure (`P01-042`).

**Interpretation.** These comments reveal **two different design tensions**: (a) deliberate hour-by-hour interaction versus effortless continuous timing, and (b) full-hour symbolic units versus the visibility of partial activity or precise timing. The supplied P01/P02 transcripts directly document tension (b); they do **not** contain validated evidence that a genuine 60-minute checkpoint improved or disrupted concentration.

**Design decision — retained.** The team will **keep a user-initiated one-hour cycle**, with an active choice to begin another session. This is an intentional design rationale: periodic participation may create a moment to reconsider whether to continue or switch. It is **not an evaluated benefit**. Any future solution for partial-hour representation should be considered separately, without silently replacing the core hourly interaction with automatic continuous recording.

**Further evaluation.** Observe at least one real-hour ending during an authentic activity to distinguish a meaningful checkpoint from an unwanted interruption. In that study, note user action *before* the facilitator asks whether they want to continue.

### T5 — Unclear state and transfer feedback weaken trust in recorded time

**Evidence.** In an unattributed retrospective account, a contributor said they could not see the Tower block drop, could not recall the expected visual change after flipping, and flipped the Token several times because they were unsure it had triggered (`P02-066`). Later, after explicit questions, a contributor interpreted symbols as instructions to flip, signs of ongoing accumulation, and readiness to start a new round (`P02-070`, `P02-072`, `P02-074`). The earlier account and later prompted interpretations should not be collapsed into a claim that first-use feedback was reliably understood.

**Interpretation.** Participants may be able to explain states **when asked about a particular pattern** yet still lack confidence during ordinary activity. In a shared recording system, uncertainty about start, completion and transfer affects whether the user believes the public trace is trustworthy.

**Design implication — proposed / verify in firmware.** Distinguish **Ready → Recording → Completed → Transfer pending → Transfer confirmed → Ready for restart** in the actual implementation, as relevant to the present automatic-transfer design. Show “transferred” only after genuine confirmation from the receiving system; do not equate a timed animation with successful upload. Evaluate first-attempt initiation and repeat flips separately from prompted pattern recognition.

### T6 — Mobility depends on activity setting and hardware, not merely Token size

**Evidence.** P01 said that Study and Gaming tokens would both sit near the same desk (`P01-056`), and that an Exercise token *might* be carried outdoors if it could work as a portable device (`P01-058`). P01 specifically identified wires as preventing outdoor use, and said the prototype could fit into a bag but was not convenient for a pocket (`P01-060`, `P01-062`). P02 reported Study/Gaming mainly at home and Exercise at a gym or pool (`P02-003`). P01 also predicted—not directly demonstrated—that attention to Tower updates would be limited while watching television (`P01-050`).

**Interpretation.** “Mobile” in this concept means recording **within different activity contexts** and reconnecting those records to a shared display. The current tethered prototype is more plausible for desk-based use than an off-site gym or outdoor session. Attention to the shared Tower may also be shaped by what people are already doing.

**Design implication — proposed.** Document power/cable constraints honestly; check whether the next version truly supports the desired physical movement; distinguish *conceptual portability* from implemented standalone operation. In subsequent studies, measure device placement, movement, line of sight and interruptions instead of inferring in-context use from hypothetical interview answers.

**Priority for the next iteration:**

1. **Clarity and integrity:** verify physical start feedback, end-of-session state and genuine receiving-device acknowledgement; provide activity/ownership/unit meanings.
2. **Experience in the shared room:** make the Tower comfortable to watch, and test how identification and disclosure choices affect the willingness to contribute.
3. **Contextual feasibility:** evaluate where tokens can actually be used and how a real one-hour ending fits an ongoing task.

**Not yet a measured outcome:** improved productivity, reduced gaming, lasting change in time allocation, voluntary household conversation, or successful reflection caused by the hourly checkpoint.

## 5. Limitations and trustworthiness

**L1 — Very small, exploratory corpus.** Two participant-labelled transcript files do not justify prevalence estimates, statistically meaningful percentages, or generalisations to all students or housemates.

**L2 — Not full verbatim recordings.** The documents were cleaned; pauses and some ambiguities were removed or corrected. Verify original audio for publication-grade direct quotes.

**L3 — Uncertain speakers.** Many important P02 remarks and several P01 role-play lines cannot be assigned to a named participant. Report them as **“unattributed staged-discussion remarks”**, not “P02 felt anxious” or “P01 asked for timestamps.”

**L4 — Facilitator effects.** Researchers explicitly described the eight-second simulation and automatic upload, requested Tower viewing and prompted discussion. Interpretations after explanation are not proof of spontaneous discoverability or social initiation.

**L5 — Simulation and time compression.** Housemate B records were simulated; eight seconds stood in for an hour. This cannot validate natural household negotiation, sustained social pressure, independent concurrent activity or interruption after a full hour.

**L6 — Partial behavioural record.** These are interview transcripts plus some written observations, not detailed time-coded video/interaction logs. Do not infer task success rates or frequency of unnoticed events where none is documented.

**L7 — Privacy and validity.** Public time traces can be misinterpreted as evidence of personal virtue, availability, productivity or a complete day. Do not publish identifiable participant comments or images beyond consent; represent activity time as a *self-selected recorded interval*, not verified performance.

For each event, capture **timestamp; prototype version; what was visible; who did what; whether the facilitator had prompted it; participant explanation; analyst interpretation; and proposed response**. Keep simulation-stage findings separate from genuine paired-use observations.

## 7. Short, poster-ready evidence statements

These statements intentionally avoid claiming effects the data do not establish. Pair each with a photo of the actual prototype or an anonymised transcript excerpt and link to the full evidence record.

> **Finding A — Shared traces are readable, but need explanation.** After being directed to the Tower, P01 recognised completed gaming units but could not identify every activity symbol (`P01-017`, `P01-021`). **Design response:** clarify the legend and time-unit semantics.

> **Finding B — Sharing changes what activity records may mean socially.** The staged shared-record discussion produced comparisons and concerns about possible judgment, while P01 valued the absence of exact start times (`P02-012`–`027`, unattributed role-play; `P01-042`). **Design response:** make privacy and non-ranking principles explicit; test real sharing.

> **Finding C — An hourly checkpoint is a design trade-off.** Discussion raised the invisibility of incomplete hours (`P02-044`, speaker pending). **Design decision:** retain an active one-hour restart to invite reconsideration, while testing whether this creates a helpful pause or an unwanted interruption. *The claimed reflective benefit has not yet been established.*

> **Finding D — Feedback must be trustworthy in context.** A contributor reported repeated flipping when unsure whether the Token had started (`P02-066`, speaker pending). **Design response:** clarify state changes and confirm successful transfer from the receiving system.

## Team-review summary — English translation of the source summary

The two source documents support a bounded conclusion: **after facilitator prompts, the Tower was used to interpret completed activity counts, categories and some sequence information, while ownership, symbol meanings, time detail and the implications of sharing remained unclear.** P01 directly linked willingness to share to the absence of exact start times (`P01-042`). The staged P02 discussion raised social comparison, possible anxiety and the need to identify contributors. Most relevant speakers remain unverified, so **these remarks must not be presented as proof that P02 personally felt anxious**.

The **active hourly interaction** is a deliberate team design decision. Discussion of the unrepresented remainder after a full hour (`P02-044`) concerns time granularity. It is related to, but distinct from, the requirement to restart actively. An eight-second demonstration cannot establish whether an actual hourly checkpoint supports reflection or interrupts activity.

Priorities are to verify clear start and genuine transfer feedback; check independent understanding of contributor identity and activity symbols; observe spontaneous discussion or avoidance in **real paired use**; and examine the Token's actual constraints at a desk, in shared space and during Exercise. Keep observations, interview accounts and implementation status separate when building the poster's **User Evidence → Design Insight → Design Response** account.
