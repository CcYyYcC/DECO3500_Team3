# Round 1 — Evaluation 01

First formative evaluation, run after the initial prototype was built.
**Three participants**, tested individually.

## What was tested

The **blue Study Token only**, with a **single-column Tower**. The other two activity Tokens
did not exist yet, so only study time could be recorded and the social layer could not be
demonstrated at all — every question about sharing concerned a shared Tower that was
*described* to the participant.

One hour was simulated as **eight seconds**, so a participant could complete two "hours"
inside a short session.

**Method:** scenario-based think-aloud, followed by a short post-test interview. Participants
were students who study in shared or semi-shared spaces, recruited from the team's own
networks — a convenience sample, and not a group who actually live together.

**Tasks:** start a study session with no instruction on how to operate the Token; complete it
and begin a second; read the Tower and say what it shows and how much time it represents;
then the interview.

## Findings

Five, written up as E1–E5 in the wiki, with E6 added from the same transcripts:

| | Finding |
|---|---|
| **E1** | A valid flip did not reliably register — a wrong gesture and an unresponsive device were indistinguishable |
| **E2** | The stack did not explain its own unit; participants understood that blocks accumulate but not what one was worth |
| **E3** | Start, timing and completion were not distinguishable at a glance |
| **E4** | A shared display changes what people are willing to record — one participant would show study and conceal gaming |
| **E5** | The hourly gesture competes with the activity it records; participants understood the flip and still would not repeat it |
| **E6** | Awareness is not the same as behaviour change |

E4 is what produced the two-column Tower. E1, E2 and E3 produced *planned* responses that
Round 2 shows are **still not resolved**.

## Limits of this round

Three participants, one activity Token, two compressed sessions, no longitudinal use. The
eight-second unit shows what happens when a session *ends*, but not what a real hourly prompt
feels like during actual work. No standardised usability score was collected, deliberately —
it would not answer whether the social concept works.
